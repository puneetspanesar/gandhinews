The Reserve Bank of India has kept **State Bank of India, HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank** on its list of domestic systemically important banks for 2026, each in the same capital bucket as a year earlier. The list, issued from Mumbai on Monday, November 23, 2026, is built on bank data as on March 31, 2026, and leaves **buckets 3 and 5 without a single lender**.

The three are the banks the regulator treats as too important to the financial system to be allowed to fail, and so asks to hold more capital than other lenders as a cushion.

## What the list says

The release places SBI in **bucket 4**, HDFC Bank in bucket 2 and ICICI Bank in bucket 1. Each bucket carries an additional Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) requirement, expressed as a share of the bank's risk-weighted assets (RWAs):

- Bucket 5: 1.00% (no bank)
- Bucket 4: 0.80% (SBI)
- Bucket 3: 0.60% (no bank)
- Bucket 2: 0.40% (HDFC Bank)
- Bucket 1: 0.20% (ICICI Bank)

The RBI says this surcharge applies **on top of the capital conservation buffer**, the cushion every bank must already keep. Banks are sorted into buckets by their Systemic Importance Scores under the D-SIB framework the RBI issued on July 22, 2014, and revised on December 28, 2023. The release states that the three banks "continue" under the same bucketing structure as the 2025 list.

## A rule for foreign banks too

The release also covers foreign banks with branches in India that are classed as global systemically important banks. They must hold an India-specific surcharge: the extra CET1 buffer their home regulator asks of the group, **multiplied by their India RWAs and divided by the group's total consolidated RWAs**. In practice the Indian branch's share of the buffer tracks its share of the global balance sheet. The release names no foreign bank.

## What it means in rupees

The release does not give rupee amounts, and neither does this report for the banks' actual books. The arithmetic is simple, though. For **every ₹10 lakh crore of risk-weighted assets**, a 0.80% surcharge means about ₹8,000 crore of additional top-quality capital, 0.40% about ₹4,000 crore and 0.20% about ₹2,000 crore. The larger a bank's balance sheet grows, the more capital the same bucket demands, which is why the buckets matter to lending capacity as well as to safety.

For depositors the practical meaning is reassurance rather than change. A bank on the list is expected to hold a thicker buffer against losses, and the list signals which institutions the regulator would treat as impossible to let collapse. The release does not say that any depositor or borrower is affected, and it announces no new rule.

## How we got here

The RBI has named such banks every year since 2015. SBI was designated first in **2015**, ICICI Bank in 2016 and HDFC Bank in 2017. In the previous list, released on November 13, 2024, the surcharges were being stepped up: SBI's was set to rise from 0.60% to 0.80% and HDFC Bank's from 0.20% to 0.40%, both from April 1, 2025, according to a report of that release. This year's list shows those higher levels in place, with no further movement.

The wider supervisory push on the same banks has not stopped. On the same day as the list, the RBI put out [draft directions on preventive safeguards for electronic banking](/en/news/rbi-stop-switch-trusted-person-draft-digital-fraud), including a stop switch for customer accounts, and Governor Sanjay Malhotra met heads of non-banking and urban cooperative lenders in Mumbai and urged responsible credit growth and stronger cybersecurity.

## What happens next

The release gives **no effective date for the requirement** and no reaction from the banks. In earlier years the higher surcharges were phased in from April 1 of the following year, but that is not stated this time and should not be assumed. The next list is due after the RBI collects bank data as on March 31, 2027, and the RBI will name the banks again then.

Until then, the three banks' buckets, and the **0.80%, 0.40% and 0.20% surcharges**, stand as the RBI has set them. A move to a higher bucket in a future list would raise the capital a lender must hold against its loans.
