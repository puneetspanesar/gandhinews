The Union Cabinet on Wednesday **approved a ₹11,000 crore scheme** to make construction and infrastructure equipment in India, aimed at machines the country now buys largely from abroad: tunnel boring machines, hydraulic excavators, ship-to-shore cranes and off-highway trucks. The Ministry of Heavy Industries will run the scheme for eight years, from 2026-27 to 2033-34.

Union Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw announced the decision after the Cabinet meeting chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, along with four other approvals worth about ₹8,900 crore, which brought the day's total to ₹19,934 crore, according to reports of the briefing. The Cabinet release issued through the Press Information Bureau on November 25 confirms the approval and the [₹11,000 crore outlay](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseDetail.aspx?PRID=2341332); the scheme terms below are as reported from the briefing.

## What the scheme pays

Money flows in two ways. The main part is a **sales-linked incentive of 13% falling to 8%** of incremental sales, paid for five years to approved makers of equipment and of components. Incremental sales are measured against a base year. The second part is a grant of **up to ₹375 crore as viability gap funding** for a dedicated testing facility, so that machines built in India can be tested and certified at home.

The calendar is back-loaded. The first two years, 2026-27 and 2027-28, are a gestation period in which companies set up plants. Incentives are paid from 2028-29 to 2032-33, and 2033-34 is kept for settling the incentive due on the last year's sales. A company that builds nothing gets nothing.

Two things are still open. Detailed guidelines on who qualifies, how the base year is fixed and what decides each firm's rate have not been published, and neither the location nor the operator of the testing facility has been disclosed. BEML and Larsen & Toubro have been named in reports as possible beneficiaries, but whether any firm qualifies depends on those guidelines.

## The import problem it targets

India's construction equipment industry is the world's third largest, after China and the United States. It was worth about ₹97,500 crore in 2024-25, and the government projects about **₹2.28 lakh crore by 2029-30**. It employs about 10 lakh people directly.

How much of that is imported depends on the count. The Indian Construction Equipment Manufacturers' Association (ICEMA) has estimated imports of about ₹34,500 crore in 2024-25, of which **₹30,250 crore was components and aggregates** such as engines, hydraulics, electronics and transmissions, and only ₹4,250 crore was finished machines. A separate figure given at the briefing, as reported, puts the import-dependent share of the market at about 45%. The scheme covers seven categories that are fully or largely imported: tunnel boring machines, off-highway trucks, excavators, cranes, loaders, piling rigs and drilling machines.

## Why the timing matters

China has tightened exports of some heavy equipment, including tunnel boring machines, walking excavators and wet shotcrete machines used at altitude, and licences from Chinese authorities are now needed, with rules reported to have taken effect on November 18. Hill, border, rail, tunnel and hydropower projects in India depend on such machines, and the curbs could also hold up spare parts, repairs and technical support for machines already in use. Two Herrenknecht machines for the Mumbai-Ahmedabad bullet train were reported to have sat at a Chinese port for over a year before reaching India in March 2026. The government has not formally linked the scheme to the curbs.

Officials say demand for tunnel boring machines is growing about 20% a year because of metro rail, high-speed rail, hydropower and mountain road projects. ICEMA wants home production of the key parts: main bearings, cutterheads, disc cutters, hydraulic systems, main drives and automation. State-owned BEML has said it plans to build tunnel boring machines in India, starting with 3.5 to 6.5 metre diameter machines for metro, hydropower, irrigation and water projects, with Indian-owned technology.

## How the scheme got here

The scheme was announced in the Union Budget for 2026-27, with a token provision of ₹200 crore. ICEMA had asked for a ₹10,000 crore production-linked scheme in July 2024. A parliamentary committee cited a proposed ₹14,300 crore over seven years in March, and a ministry communication on November 5 said the Expenditure Finance Committee had recommended a revised outlay of ₹11,000 crore, the figure the Cabinet cleared. Heavy Industries Minister H D Kumaraswamy told ICEMA's annual conference in New Delhi that the aim is to strengthen domestic manufacturing and reduce dependence on imported components and machines.

## What happens next

The Ministry of Heavy Industries must now issue operational guidelines and open applications. Until then, no company has a confirmed incentive, and the first payments are not due before 2028-29. How many firms take part, and how much of the ₹97,500 crore market they can win from imports, will show in applications and in the plants announced over the next two years.
