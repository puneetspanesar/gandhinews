Union Minister of State Jitendra Singh said on Tuesday that India's space economy, **worth about $9 billion today**, is likely to reach $40-45 billion in less than a decade, with private companies driving the change. He was speaking at the fifth India International Space Conclave in New Delhi, held on November 23 and 24 by the Indian Space Association (ISpA).

Singh, who is minister of state with independent charge for science and technology and earth sciences, and minister of state in the Prime Minister's Office and the departments of atomic energy and space, said the ecosystem had **grown from single-digit startups to roughly 400-500 companies** in a few years. He credited the reforms that opened the sector to private firms.

## What the minister said

The Department of Space, in a release issued at 5:13 pm on Tuesday, said Singh called the creation of New Space India Limited (NSIL), the later setting up of IN-SPACe, and the opening of the sector to private participation **a decisive shift** in India's space journey. In his words as reported by the department, the changes were among the most transformative policy decisions of recent decades, because they broke the status quo and let private enterprise take part in a field that had been largely confined to government.

He pointed to Skyroot Aerospace as proof. The startup's first launch vehicle flew on **July 18, 2026**, and Singh said its multi-mission launch agreement and its unicorn status were significant milestones. He also named Agnikul and other firms building advanced propulsion systems, privately made rocket engines, Earth observation technology and satellite imaging capability. Companies from other industries are now joining as well, he said, which shows the commercial openings the reforms have created.

Singh argued that the economic value of space should not be measured only by rocket launches, satellite manufacturing and commercial revenue. It should also count the savings, efficiency and better results it produces on the ground in other sectors. Citing the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan, he said space-based applications help plan infrastructure, keep projects on schedule, **cut costs and reduce leakage**. Almost every ministry, from railways and defence to health, education and communications, now uses space technology, he said.

## Asked about demand and sovereignty

In the question-and-answer session, Singh was asked how to create more demand for the space industry. He said the perceived lack of demand stems mainly from **too little awareness** of what space technology can do in practice, and that the opportunities in infrastructure, communications and delivery of public services go well beyond launches and satellites. He described the prime minister's whole-of-government approach and cooperation between ministries as important in widening its use.

Asked how to balance strategic sovereignty with international cooperation, he said the two are not in conflict. India should keep strengthening its own capabilities, with a focus on indigenous sources, technology and projects, while staying open to partnerships that benefit both sides.

He also cited the National Quantum Mission, launched in 2023 with an eight-year term. Its core target is secure quantum communication over 2,000 km in eight years, and India has already reached **1,000 km in three years**, he said. Space, quantum technologies, semiconductors and atomic science are interlinked, he added, and each offers openings for technical progress and commercial growth.

## The appeal to industry

Singh urged domestic private investors to put more money into space and asked industry leaders to help bring in more foreign investment. He said the government had set up supporting arrangements, including the Anusandhan National Research Foundation and the Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) fund, to strengthen research and encourage private participation. He also asked companies to give more under corporate social responsibility (CSR) for research and scientific development, saying CSR should reach the wider community and not stop at machinery and infrastructure spending.

At the conclave, a report titled "Space Economy 2036: The India Blueprint" was also released. ISRO chairman and Department of Space secretary V Narayanan, IN-SPACe chairman Pawan Kumar Goenka and ISpA director general Lt Gen Anil Kumar Bhatt (retd) were among those present. The release does not describe the report's findings.

## How we got here

The sector's opening is the backdrop to Singh's numbers. NSIL was created as ISRO's commercial arm, and IN-SPACe followed as the body that authorises and supports private activity. In October, Narayanan told graduates in Chennai that ISRO would [have four working launch pads by 2029](/news/isro-four-launch-pads-2029-narayanan-not-privatised) and would not be privatised, but would work with private companies and startups as partners to meet demand for satellites. All of India's launches today go from Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh.

Private players are also looking to serve customers directly. Satellite broadband is one area where the government has said every licensed company must clear the same security process, as we reported when [Starlink was still waiting for its clearance](/news/starlink-waits-on-security-clearance-as-musk-row-escalates).

## Why it matters

The figures are a minister's projection, not a forecast backed by published workings, and the release gives no year for the $40-45 billion mark beyond "less than a decade". For readers, the stake is jobs and contracts in a young industry. Singh's pitch is that demand will come from government users such as railways, defence and infrastructure planners, who he said already rely on space data. Singh himself said that perceived weak demand comes down to low awareness of what the technology can do.

## What next

The government has not announced new funds or rules at the conclave. Watch for the contents of the "Space Economy 2036" report, which is the first detailed account of how the industry arrives at its figures, and for ISRO's launch schedule, which sets how much work private firms can bid for in the coming year.
