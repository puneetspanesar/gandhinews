On Wednesday the Centre told the Supreme Court that the investigation into the crash of Air India flight AI171 is at "the fag end". It asked for **two to three more weeks**. The bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant agreed. The Solicitor General, Tushar Mehta, said there was "no reason why we should delay deliberately".

We take him at his word on intent. But the plain arithmetic is hard to defend. The Boeing 787 went down seconds after take-off from Ahmedabad on June 12, 2025, and **260 people died**. More than 17 months later there is no final report. The court was told in July that it would come in the autumn. The autumn target has passed.

## What the families have and have not got

The only public document is the 15-page preliminary report of July 12, 2025. It said the fuel control switches of both engines moved from RUN to CUTOFF within seconds of lift-off, and that one pilot asked the other why he had cut off the fuel. It recommended nothing for operators. That means the final report is **the first place any safety recommendation can appear**.

The petitioners, who include the father of the pilot in command and a pilots' body, want an independent, transparent inquiry. In July the court put that demand on hold until the final report arrives. So the families wait, and the court waits, on a single document.

## The fair objection

The investigators have a point. Passengers of several nationalities were on board, which brought foreign experts into the work, and some components had to go to manufacturers abroad. Under international practice a draft is shared with the states taking part before release. A rushed report that gets the cause wrong would be worse than a late one. The investigation bureau also says the law gives it an "absolute statutory prohibition" on sharing cockpit voice and image recordings with anyone outside, and that is a rule to be tested in court, not by pressure alone.

None of that explains why the public has been given no timetable. Care and openness are not opposites.

## What should happen now

First, the Centre should come to the next hearing with **a fixed date for publication**, not a further estimate, and the court should record it in its order.

Second, the bureau should say in plain words what stage the report is at: whether the draft has gone to the foreign states, whether their comments are in, and what remains. A process the public can see is easier to trust than one described as nearly finished.

Third, if the probe has found anything that bears on how the fleet is flown or maintained, an interim safety notice should go out now. Other crews are flying the same type today. Waiting for the last page of a report to protect them is a choice, and a poor one.

Fourth, the objection to the make-up of the investigating team, which the petitioners have raised in court, should be answered on the record. The Centre should say who sits on the team and why it considers it independent.

Finally, the report should be released **to the families first**, in full, and at the same time as to the public. They have waited longest.

Seventeen months is not a record for a complex inquiry. But a country that asks passengers to trust its airlines and its regulator owes them an investigation that visibly finishes. The court has asked for the report. The next hearing should end with a date.
