When a government puts a number like **₹9,321 crore** in a press note, the first instinct of a reader is to be glad. Rajasthan has had a poor monsoon, about 20% less rain than its average, and on November 13 Chief Minister Bhajan Lal Sharma declared 87 tehsils in 15 districts drought-affected, 73 of them severely. A package was due. The question is what is in it.

## What the state has said

The instructions that came out of the review meeting are sensible. Fodder depots are to run in districts with below-normal rain, and gaushalas are to get water and fodder. The Agriculture Department must prepare a loss report on kharif crops through the girdawari, the field-by-field survey, and farmers who lost crops are to get relief. Tubewells are to get priority for power, and rural wage work is to be speeded up.

All of that is a list of tasks. **None of the reports of the meeting says how the ₹9,321 crore is divided** between fodder, drinking water, crop-loss compensation and wages. They do not say whether it is fresh money or the total of existing schemes and disaster funds, and they do not say how much the state expects from the Centre.

## Why the split matters

A drought package is only as good as its weakest line. If most of it is the cost of schemes that were running anyway, a farmer in Jaisalmer or Barmer has been promised little that is new. If it is mostly crop-loss compensation, it is useless to a family whose problem next month is a cow that cannot be fed.

Western Rajasthan has already shown how fast a dry year turns into a livestock crisis. On October 18, former Chief Minister Ashok Gehlot said thousands of cattle had died in Jaisalmer and that fodder was selling at ₹20 a kg. Those are his claims, and the state has published no count. That is the point: without numbers on animals, depots and tankers, nobody outside the government can tell whether the package is catching up with the need.

## The fair view

The government has a good answer to some of this. Declaring tehsils, ordering depots and starting the survey are the right first steps, and a detailed budget cannot be written before the loss survey is done. Relief that waits for a perfect schedule helps nobody. Sums of this size are also normally announced as an envelope and filled in later.

That is true, and it is why the missing pieces should be easy to supply. An envelope can be published with its heads. A survey can be given a deadline.

## What Rajasthan should publish

First, a head-wise breakdown of the **₹9,321 crore**: how much for fodder and cattle, for drinking water, for crop-loss relief, for wages, and for power to tubewells.

Second, a statement of how much is new. Money already in the budget or the state disaster response fund is not a package, and farmers deserve to know the difference.

Third, **a payment calendar**. The girdawari report should have a closing date, and relief per farmer should follow within a fixed number of days. The reports say the assessment is to cover tenant farmers as well as landowners; that should be written into the order, since tenants are the ones most often left out.

Fourth, a weekly public count for each declared tehsil: fodder depots open, tankers running, tubewell connections given, and cattle lost. If the Centre is being asked for help, the sum should be said aloud.

The monsoon fell short. Winter is when ponds are empty and fodder runs out. A farmer in a declared tehsil needs to know three things: what he will get, from which scheme, and on which date. Until the state says so, the package is a promise with a number on it.
