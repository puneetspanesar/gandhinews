The headline from the Indian Council of Medical Research is a reassuring one. In its SAMPADA survey of more than 2 lakh people, **41.8% of children aged 12 to 59 months** were anaemic, against 67.1% of children aged 6 to 59 months in the last National Family Health Survey. Among women aged 15 to 49 the figure is **55.9%, against 57%**. ICMR's director general, Dr Rajiv Bahl, called the lower prevalence "encouraging".

It may be. But a fall of 25 points among children is exactly the kind of number that gets quoted in a speech before anyone has checked what produced it.

## Two tests, one comparison

NFHS-5, fielded in 2019-21, measured haemoglobin from a finger-prick drop of blood on a portable device. SAMPADA drew blood from a vein and ran it on automated analysers in field laboratories within four to six hours, which ICMR calls the gold standard. The age bands differ as well: 6 to 59 months then, 12 to 59 months now, and 15 to 19 years for adolescent girls then against 10 to 19 now.

The ICMR release says only that prevalence is lower than in previous national surveys. It does not say how much of the gap is real. The lower numbers may reflect the new method as well as any change in children's health, and **the published data do not separate the two**. That is the honest reading, and it should be said plainly each time the figures are used.

## The case for taking the good news

There is a fair counter-argument. Venous testing is more accurate, the health ministry told Parliament in July that it had dropped haemoglobin testing from NFHS-6 for exactly that reason, and Anaemia Mukt Bharat has run since 2018. Officials could reasonably say the programme deserves some credit, and that waiting for a perfect comparison means waiting for years. Reading a lower figure as no news at all would be as wrong as reading it as a victory.

The point is narrower. A programme can be defended with evidence, and at present the evidence cannot tell us how much of the fall is the programme.

## Where the numbers are not in doubt

Some findings need no bridge to be alarming. **Over half of Indian women of childbearing age are anaemic**, and 46.3% of adolescent girls. In Andhra Pradesh 57.5% of young children are anaemic, against 14.5% in Kerala. About half of the cases in women, girls and children come from iron deficiency, which means the rest do not, and the report points to infections, inherited blood disorders and chronic inflammation. As the desk's news report noted, iron tablets alone will not close that gap.

## What should happen now

First, ICMR and the health ministry should publish a **bridge between the two methods**: a technical note, using samples tested both ways if they exist, that estimates how far the finger-prick figures overstated anaemia. Without it, no comparison with NFHS-5 should be presented as a trend.

Second, SAMPADA should become the new baseline, with a published date for its next round using the same test, so that real change can finally be measured. The release gives no such date, and an RTI reply dated September 1 gave none for the first results either.

Third, Anaemia Mukt Bharat should be reviewed against the new baseline, with state-level targets, especially where the survey shows the highest prevalence. ICMR-NIN's own director, Dr Bharati Kulkarni, has called for targeted, population-specific approaches across a person's life. The programme's design should show that it has listened.

India has waited years for trustworthy anaemia numbers, and now has them. The next step is to say honestly what they can and cannot show.
