## What is a sea mine?

**It is an explosive charge in a casing that waits for a ship to come to it.** Navies have used mines for over a century because they are cheap, hard to see and can close a waterway without a single warship. One reported estimate puts the cost of a modern mine at about **$35,000**, against a tanker and its cargo that can be worth around $300 million.

There are four common kinds. **Bottom mines** rest on the seabed in shallow water and can look like rocks or debris. **Moored mines** are tied to the seabed by a cable and float just under the surface. **Drifting mines** are loose and move with currents, which makes them the least predictable. **Limpet mines** are small devices clamped to a hull, usually on a timer.

Most are set off in one of two ways: by contact, or by an "influence" sensor that reacts to a ship's magnetic, acoustic or pressure signature as it passes. Influence mines are harder to detect and can go off without any touch.

## How much do we know about Hormuz?

**Less than the headlines suggest.** Analysts' estimates of Iran's stockpile run from 2,000 to 6,000 mines including traditional moored contact mines and seabed influence mines, many made in Iran. The Revolutionary Guards have kept most of their small mine-laying boats, so more could be laid in hours.

Iran's claims on Saturday, November 14, are that three tankers that had switched off their tracking systems hit mines and caught fire, one in a zone east of the strait that it calls prohibited. They have not been independently confirmed, and no names, flags or casualty figures have been given. The United States says it has cleared the main routes. Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi disputed an earlier clearance claim in August, saying: "No one except Iran knows the location of these mines."

## Why are mines so slow to clear?

**Because finding one mine is hard, and proving there are no more is harder.** The method has three steps.

First, **mine hunting**: ships, helicopters and underwater drones with sonar scan the seabed, and an operator must decide whether each contact is a mine or clutter. Second, **neutralisation**: a diver or a remotely operated robot places a small charge on the mine, which is detonated from a safe distance. Third, **sweeping**: ships tow devices that mimic a vessel's magnetic and acoustic signature to trigger influence mines harmlessly, or cutters that snag a moored mine's cable so it floats up to be destroyed.

Every suspicious object is examined one by one, and sweeps are repeated. The US Navy's dedicated mine-countermeasure force had been run down over the years, and **its MH-53E minehunting helicopters were retired in August**. Washington relies more on drones and warships, and Britain and France have led a separate effort. Loose mines can sit outside the monitored lanes, and new ones can be laid. Maritime warning centres have kept the strait's mine-danger areas active.


## What do mines do to insurance?

**They raise the price of every voyage, whether or not a ship is hit.** Ordinary marine insurance excludes war. Shipowners buy separate war-risk cover for a trip through a danger zone, and it is priced as a share of the ship's value for each transit.

According to an industry report in July, that rate in Hormuz rose from **1 to 3% of a ship's hull value to 7.5 to 10%**. Experts said premiums would stay high until there was a long stretch of calm.

Before the war about 130 ships a day passed through the strait, carrying roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and gas. On a supertanker worth about $300 million with its cargo, those rates work out at **$22.5 million to $30 million a transit**, up from $3 million to $9 million, a cost that is passed on in freight and in the price of oil.

## Why does it matter to India?

**India buys a large share of its crude through Hormuz.** According to the ship-tracking figures in our report on Saturday's claims, India took 2.1 million barrels a day from the Gulf in November so far. Brent settled at $95.89 a barrel on Friday, against about $72 before the war. Each new fire on the route lifts insurance and freight costs for those cargoes, and Indian seafarers are exposed whatever flag a ship flies.

## What happens next?

A mine-free strait will not be declared quickly. Even after any deal, lanes would need to be searched again and again before insurers and shipowners call them safe. For now the tests are practical: whether the three tankers are identified, whether US or allied minehunters report more finds, and whether war-risk rates start to fall.
