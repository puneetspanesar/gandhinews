India's trade negotiators have spent much of this year waiting for an American election. Officials were reported in April to want to see the shape of the next Congress before making tariff commitments. On Wednesday they got their answer: **Democrats won both chambers**, taking the House and the Senate from President Donald Trump's Republicans for his last two years in office.

It is tempting to read that as relief for Indian exporters. It is not, or not yet. The result removes the reason for waiting. It does not remove the tariff.

## The tariff does not need Congress

Indian goods entering the United States pay **a 10% duty under Section 301** of the Trade Act of 1974, in force since 24 July. It came out of a US investigation into goods made with forced labour, and India was given the lower of two rates after it changed its own trade rules in June. Unlike the emergency tariffs the US Supreme Court struck down in February, this one rests on a law that lets the President act alone, and it has no fixed end date.

A Democratic Congress can hold hearings, demand documents and ask why American shoppers are paying more. Voters plainly want that: seven in ten told exit pollsters they wanted Congress to act as more of a check on the administration, and prices topped their worries. But a bill to cut tariffs would still need Trump's signature, and overriding his veto takes two-thirds of both chambers. Max Yoeli of Chatham House put it well before the vote: opposition on foreign policy "will be more about optics than hard constraints".

## Democrats are not softer on Russian oil

The bigger threat to Indian exports is the Russia sanctions law Trump signed on 18 September, which allows **tariffs of up to 100%** on the largest buyers of Russian oil and gas, India among them. That law was not a Republican project alone. Its co-author, Democratic Senator Richard Blumenthal, said after the House vote: "To China and India: you better clean up your act. Buy your oil and gas somewhere else."

Hakeem Jeffries, now in line to be Speaker, opposed that bill because of what it would cost American households. That is a useful opening for India. It is not a promise. Trump waived the penalties in October, and he can lift the waiver whenever he likes. A Democratic Senate will press him to be tougher on Moscow, not softer.

## The fair objection

Some will say India should hold out longer. A weakened President, facing a hostile Congress and voters angry about prices, may need a trade win more than India does, and better terms could come later.

Perhaps. But exporters cannot price goods on perhaps. Every month of uncertainty is a month in which buyers hedge their orders. And the first tranche of the deal is, by Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal's own account in July, already finalised. He said it would take effect "as soon as the United States is able to ensure that India gets a comparative advantage over its competitors". Secretary of State Marco Rubio said last month that the two sides were "very close". The gap is narrow enough to close.

## What New Delhi should do

First, **close the first tranche now**, on terms India can defend at home, and with a rate that keeps Indian goods ahead of competitors such as Vietnam and China, which pay 12.5% under the same duty.

Second, keep the later stages of the deal clear of anything that would need a fresh vote in Congress unless India is sure of support from both parties. A deal that depends on one party's goodwill can be undone by the next.

Third, start talking to the new majority now. Committee chairs who will hold hearings on tariffs, visas and Russia sanctions from January should hear India's case directly, including on the $100,000 fee on new H-1B petitions, which a federal judge in California blocked in September and which the administration is appealing.

Fourth, keep widening India's options. Goods exports to the US rose to **$42.79 billion between April and August**, up from $40.39 billion a year earlier, even with the duty. That resilience is a strength, but no single market, and no single election, should decide India's trade policy.

Washington has changed. The tariff on Indian goods has not. India should negotiate with the government that sets it, and talk to the Congress that will question it.
