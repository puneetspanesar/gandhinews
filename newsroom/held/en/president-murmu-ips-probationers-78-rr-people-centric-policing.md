President Droupadi Murmu told young Indian Police Service officers on Tuesday that **the public sees the police as the face of governance**, and that their real respect will come from how they work and what values they live by, not from the powers the law gives them.

The probationers of the **78 RR (2025 batch)** called on the President at Rashtrapati Bhavan in New Delhi on November 10, 2026, according to a release from the President's Secretariat issued at 3:21 pm.

## What the President said

Ms Murmu said that where policing is good, people say the administration is good. When an officer hears a complaint with care and sensitivity, she said, the person in front of them feels **a great sense of support**. She asked the officers to give priority to removing fear from the minds of people who are weak socially or economically and who cannot reach senior officials, and to solving their problems.

The President reminded the probationers that the laws and rules of policing will give them wide powers. Public respect, she said, has to be earned through performance and values. Police officers, she said, should carry **a spirit of serving citizens**, not a wish to show their own superiority over them.

On running a team, she said some staff may be better than senior officers at using technology, and she advised the trainees **not to hesitate to learn from the most junior members** of their teams. She urged them to encourage public participation and teamwork, and said that by putting the welfare of their team members first they would raise the abilities and enthusiasm of those colleagues.

## Cyber crime, deepfakes and misinformation

Much of the address dealt with new kinds of crime. Ms Murmu said **cyber crime is growing wider and more serious**. The police, she said, will have to create awareness across society of how grave it is, and police officers must change their own approach to it.

She called deepfakes a new kind of threat to people and said officers must always stay **one step ahead of criminals who use the latest technology**.

She also named a second problem: false information spread to create fear. Impressionable people, particularly the young, can be misled through planned propaganda, she said, and such acts can be organised at local, regional or national level. In her view, smart policing can stop such situations from becoming very big and very difficult to handle.

## Where this sits

The batch finished the first phase of its training at the national police academy in Hyderabad last month, when Union Home Secretary Govind Mohan told the 151 trainees that drugs, cybercrime and illegal immigration were the new tests. We reported that on [October 17](/en/news/home-secretary-govind-mohan-ips-passing-out-security). The President's remarks pick up the same theme from the side of public conduct: cyber crime and misinformation, and how officers treat the people who come to them.

## Why it matters

For a citizen, the officer at the police station or the cyber cell is the first and often the only door for a complaint. The President's emphasis on listening to people with no access to senior officers, and on treating cyber crime as a serious offence that needs public awareness, is a direction aimed at that first contact. The advice on learning from junior staff reflects how much of the technical work in a police unit often rests with its younger members, as the President herself noted.

## What happens next

The release sets no deadline or programme. The next public marker on the cyber fraud front is the Supreme Court hearing on **November 23, 2026**, by which time the RBI must list all authorised financial institutions.
