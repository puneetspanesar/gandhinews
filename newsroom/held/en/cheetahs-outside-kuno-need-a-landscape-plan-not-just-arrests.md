The young male known as KNG-22 was 19 months old, born in India to Nirva, one of the cheetahs flown in from South Africa. He was found badly hurt on 25 October in a territorial forest outside Kuno National Park, and moved to Jabalpur, where surgeons **removed five firearm pellets**. "The male cheetah could not be saved," Chief Wildlife Warden Sameeta Rajora said two days later.

By Monday two brothers from Morena had been sent to jail by a court in Vijaypur. Investigators say one of them admitted firing at the cat with a licensed gun while the two were hunting in the forest, and that the killing does not look like the work of a poaching gang. Four forest staff have been **suspended for lapses in monitoring**.

Swift arrests are welcome. They should not let anyone believe the problem has been solved.

## The cats were always going to leave

Project Cheetah began in September 2022 with eight animals from Namibia, then twelve from South Africa in February 2023 and nine from Botswana this February. India now has **58 cheetahs, 52 of them at Kuno**, four at Gandhi Sagar and two at Veerangana Durgavati.

The point of the project is a wild population, and a wild population moves. Young cheetahs separate from their mothers at about 18 months, roughly KNG-22's age, and go looking for space of their own. Kuno alone cannot hold that. Some of the country's leading wildlife biologists, Ravi Chellam among them, have long argued that **the park is too small** for animals that range so widely.

So the forests of Sheopur and Morena, the fields between them and the highways that cut through them are not the edge of the project. They are where it is being tested. Last December a cub that had strayed out of Kuno was **killed by a vehicle on the Agra-Mumbai highway** near Gwalior. Now one has been shot.

## The fair objection

It can be argued that no monitoring system can follow every animal through every village, and that one man with a gun cannot be blamed on a programme. Radio collars told investigators where KNG-22 had been; they helped find the accused. Villages around Kuno have so far lived with cheetahs that wander in and sometimes take a calf.

That is true, and it is exactly why the next step matters. Tolerance runs out when losses go unpaid and when a cat in a field is treated as somebody else's problem.

## What should change

First, the Centre's committee, which includes officials of the National Tiger Conservation Authority and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, should **publish its findings**, including how long it took field teams to reach the injured animal and why.

Second, Madhya Pradesh should treat the territorial forests around Kuno as part of the project, with staff, vehicles and response teams to match, not leave them to divisions already stretched thin.


Third, villages within the cheetahs' range need **compensation paid within days**, not months, for livestock lost to the cats, and a phone number that brings a team when a cheetah turns up. People who report an animal should be thanked, not questioned.

Fourth, the licensing and checking of guns in the villages around the park deserves a hard look. A licensed weapon used for hunting in a forest is a failure of enforcement long before it reaches the cheetah.

Finally, the corridor to Gandhi Sagar and beyond, which runs along the Rajasthan border, should be planned openly with both states, so that young animals moving out have somewhere safe to go.

India brought cheetahs back more than seven decades after declaring them extinct. Keeping them alive outside the fence is the real work of Project Cheetah.
