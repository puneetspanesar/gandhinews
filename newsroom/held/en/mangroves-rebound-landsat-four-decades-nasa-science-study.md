Mangrove forests, which lost almost 3,000 square kilometres of cover in the late twentieth century, have been growing back across the world, according to satellite maps NASA's Earth Observatory featured on Friday, November 13, 2026.

The maps cover **1984 to 2023** and come from a study in the journal *Science* led by Tulane University. Using decades of Landsat imagery, the researchers tracked the extent and density of mangrove canopy year by year, in what NASA describes as the first global maps of the trees over so long a period.

## What the maps show

Between 1984 and 2010, mangroves lost **nearly 3,000 sq km**, about 1,200 square miles. After 2010 the trend turned. The world gained back **nearly 2,000 sq km** of mangrove, and many forests that survived now have denser canopies than they did in 2010, especially in South America. A release from the University of Cambridge, which took part in the work, puts the net decline over the whole four decades at about 1%, far smaller than earlier estimates had suggested.

Most of the recovery is new growth rather than repair. According to NASA, **two-thirds of the expansion** came from mangroves colonising ground where they had not stood before, such as the mouth of the Amazon River and northern Australia, where the trend runs back to the 1990s. The remaining third came from regrowth in forests that had been degraded.

"About two-thirds of the expansion was due to mangroves colonizing new areas," said Zhen Zhang, an environmental scientist at Tulane University and the lead author.

## Myanmar, loss and recovery

Myanmar's coast shows the full swing. Farming, shrimp and fish ponds and firewood cutting in the 1980s and 1990s removed **28.6% of the country's mangrove area** between the 1980s and 2010. Since then the area has grown by about 10%, with the Myeik District among the places where the forests expanded.

NASA and the researchers point to several reasons for the turnaround. Awareness of mangroves grew after the 2004 tsunami, which prompted restoration. Stronger legal protection for forests helped. Sediment piling up created new mudflats where seedlings could take hold, and warmer winters with fewer freezes let mangroves creep to the edges of their range.

## Where losses continue

The recovery is uneven. Loss has continued since 2010 in the Niger Delta and across parts of West and Central Africa. In North America and southern Africa, tropical cyclones knocked back forests. The Cambridge release notes that a severe freeze in Texas in 2021 caused a sharp decline, and that newly grown stands are young and give fewer benefits than mature ones.

Zhang said the gains should not be read as a reason to relax. "The global successes we've seen with mangroves during the past decade should not be seen as grounds for complacency," he said. He added, in the Cambridge release, that "the most immediate and effective way to protect mangroves is to stop deforestation."

## Why it matters

Mangroves grow to about 13 metres on average, far shorter than the trees of inland tropical forests, yet NASA says their ecosystems **store up to four times as much carbon**.

"After decades of loss, we're finally seeing a global turning point for mangroves," Zhang said in the Cambridge release. Thomas Worthington of Cambridge called the findings "a conservation success story", while noting that "there are still areas of concern where losses are ongoing."

The NASA feature does not discuss India or give country-by-country figures beyond Myanmar, so how the Indian coast fares in the maps is not reported here.
