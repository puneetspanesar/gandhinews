## What is the World Economic Outlook?

**It is the International Monetary Fund's main forecast for the global economy, published twice a year with a growth and inflation estimate for almost every country.**

The full report comes out each April and October, usually around the IMF's spring and annual meetings. Shorter updates, with revised headline numbers but less analysis, follow in January and July. The October 2026 edition was released on Tuesday.

The forecasts are prepared by the Fund's economists, who work on each country with the same global assumptions about oil prices, interest rates and world trade. That common base is what makes the report useful: it lets readers compare India with China, Brazil or Germany on equal terms.

## What does it say about India this time?

**Growth of 7% in 2026-27 and again in 2027-28, an upgrade from 6.4% and 6.7% in July.**

The Fund said India "remains among the fastest-growing major economies", pointing to "stronger-than-expected momentum, supported by resilient domestic demand and robust services activity". The economy grew 7.8% in April-June. The IMF expects consumer inflation to average 5% this year, up from 2% in 2025-26.

For the world, it sees growth of 3.1% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027, and warns that "risks are skewed to the downside".

## How does that compare with the RBI?

**Very closely. The RBI projects 7.1% growth for 2026-27 and inflation of 5.2%, a shade above the IMF on both.**

The Reserve Bank's Monetary Policy Committee published those figures on 7 October, when it raised the repo rate to 5.50%. Other forecasters are in the same range: the World Bank also says 7.1%, and the Asian Development Bank 7%.

## Why don't the numbers match exactly?

**Because each forecaster works to a different purpose, a different timetable and its own assumptions.**

The RBI forecasts in order to set interest rates. It updates its numbers at each of its six policy meetings a year, draws on its own surveys of households and companies, and breaks the year into quarters. It now expects growth of 7.2% in July-September, slowing to 6.9% and 6.8% in the next two quarters.

The IMF forecasts to compare countries and warn about global risks. It revises India less often, and its oil and interest-rate assumptions are set for the whole world at one time, which may be weeks before the report appears. A tenth of a percentage point between the two is well within the margin any forecaster would admit to.

## Is the IMF's year the same as India's?

**Yes, for India.** Most countries in the report are measured by calendar year, but the IMF presents India's figures on the Indian financial year, April to March. When the Fund says India will grow 7% "in 2026", it means 2026-27, the same period the RBI is forecasting. This is a common source of confusion in headlines.

## Why do the forecasts keep changing?

**Because the economy keeps surprising forecasters, in both directions.**

In July the IMF cut its India number, worried that higher energy prices would slow growth. Three months later, after a strong first quarter, it reversed that cut. The RBI's own estimate at the end of August was reported at 6.7% before it moved to 7.1%. Revisions are a sign that forecasters are updating on new data, not that the earlier figure was careless.

## What could pull the numbers down?

**Mainly oil, war and a possible shake-out in artificial intelligence investment.**

The IMF has modelled a case where crude stays above $130 a barrel through the first quarter of 2027. In that case world growth next year would be 0.5 percentage points lower, and energy importers such as India would be among the hardest hit. It also warned that a sharp correction in AI investment could cut global output by 1.1% by 2028. "The current technology tailwind could stop abruptly," the report said.

For India, which imports most of its crude, oil prices are the variable to watch. Brent has traded above $100 a barrel this week.

## Why should anyone outside economics care?

**Because these numbers shape real decisions.** Growth forecasts feed into the government's tax estimates and borrowing plans, the RBI's choices on interest rates and therefore on loan EMIs, and how foreign investors and rating agencies view India. When the IMF, the RBI and the World Bank all land near 7%, it gives a fairly settled picture of where the economy is heading, at least until the next oil shock.
