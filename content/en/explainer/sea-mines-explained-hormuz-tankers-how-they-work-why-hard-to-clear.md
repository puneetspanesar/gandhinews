## What is a sea mine?

**It is an explosive charge placed in the water that detonates when a ship hits it or passes close enough to set off its sensors.** It needs no crew, no radar and no missile launcher. Once laid, it waits.

That patience is the point. A mine does the work of a warship without being one, and it can threaten a vessel of any flag, friend or enemy, long after the person who laid it has gone.

## What kinds are there?

**Three types matter in the Gulf**, according to analysts who have studied Iran's stocks.

*Contact mines* are anchored to the seabed with a cable and float below the surface. They explode when a hull strikes them. Iran's M-08, a design that goes back to the First World War, is of this type. *Influence mines*, also called bottom mines, rest on the seabed and sense a ship's magnetic, acoustic or pressure signature. The ship need not touch them. Iran's Maham-2 is an example. *Rocket mines*, the most advanced, are moored as deep as 200 metres and fire a rocket at the underside of a ship that passes overhead. Reports cite Chinese EM-52 mines.

## How many does Iran have?

**Maritime analysts estimate 2,000 to 6,000**, many made at home. A sophisticated mine costs tens of thousands of dollars, according to the Foreign Policy Research Institute, while the economic damage it causes is many times larger. Iran has never publicly confirmed laying any or said where they are. The Revolutionary Guards have said only that anti-ship mines are likely in the main traffic zone, and in April they published a map pushing ships towards a route closer to the Iranian coast.

## The latest claims

The Guards said late on Saturday that **two tankers on an "unauthorised route"** in the eastern strait struck mines, with their tracking systems switched off, and could not sail on. By Sunday they said both ships were on fire. The Guards had made similar claims on November 4, when they said four tankers were destroyed, and on November 6. Neither the UK's maritime trade body nor the US Central Command had confirmed the latest claim when reports were filed, and the names, flags and crews were not given.

## Why are mines so hard to clear?

**Because finding them is slow, and being sure that none remain is close to impossible.** Clearing involves "mine hunting" with high-resolution sonar and underwater drones, and "minesweeping" with mechanical or magnetic gear. Small clearance ships are fragile and need destroyers and aircraft to guard them against shore-based missiles and fast boats.

The US began clearing on April 11, using the destroyers USS Frank E Petersen Jr and USS Michael Murphy. On April 21 Pentagon officials told a House committee that full clearance **could take six months**. Meanwhile the US Navy's dedicated mine-hunting force had shrunk: it retired its last four Avenger-class mine-countermeasure ships in Bahrain in September, and its MH-53E Sea Dragon minesweeping helicopters were phased out in August.

On August 25 President Donald Trump said the Navy had removed or detonated all the mines. Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi replied the next day that the strait remained closed and that "no one except Iran knows the location of these mines." Independent analysts such as Mark Hilborne of King's College London found the American account credible, but warned that **loose "ghost" mines** could still lurk outside the main lanes and that Iran could simply lay new ones.

## Why does the fear matter more than the mine?

**One suspected mine is enough to stop traffic.** "The mined area does not have to be everywhere to be everywhere in the minds of those who must transit it," retired Romanian naval officer Alexandru Cristian Hudisteanu said in April.

Insurance is where this bites. Before the war, war-risk cover for a ship cost mostly under 0.25% of its hull value. In April quotes ran at 1% to 5%, which for a $100 million ship means about $5 million a transit instead of roughly $250,000. By July S&P Global put the range at 7.5% to 10%. A supertanker and its cargo can be worth about $300 million, so owners want proof of safety, not hope. Oscar Seikaly of NSI Insurance Group said the market "can insure volatility, but it struggles to insure uncertainty."

## What it means for India

Before the war about 130 ships a day passed through the strait. India imports about 88% of its crude oil, so a strait that stays closed or costly to insure keeps pressure on fuel prices and freight bills. Any lasting reopening needs three things at once: a ceasefire the insurers believe, a cleared and surveyed channel, and no fresh mines. For now, the Guards' claims, the American blockade of Iranian ports and the lack of a settlement mean none of the three is in place.
