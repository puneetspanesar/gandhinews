## How does New Zealand vote?

**Every voter casts two votes: one for a local MP, one for a party.** The country is divided into **71 electorates** (some of them reserved Māori electorates), and the winner in each takes a seat. The second vote, for a party, is the one that sets the balance in Parliament.

Parliament normally has **120 seats**. Of these, 71 go to electorate winners and the other 49 are filled from party lists, so that each party's total seats match its share of the party vote as closely as possible. The system is called Mixed Member Proportional, or MMP.

## Who gets seats at all?

**A party needs 5% of the party vote or one electorate seat.** Below that, its votes count for nothing in the seat tally. This year the newcomer Opportunity Party stood at about 6% on election night, over the line, and is set to enter Parliament for the first time with around seven seats, by its own claim. Te Pāti Māori took only 0.6% of the party vote but is likely to return at least four MPs because it wins electorates.

## What is an overhang?

**It is a seat a party wins locally that its party vote does not entitle it to.** If a party's electorate wins exceed its proportional share, it keeps all of them, and the House simply gets bigger. In 2026 the early count showed National and Te Pāti Māori with such seats, so Parliament may grow to **130 seats, the largest in the country's history**.

This changes the arithmetic. With 130 seats, a government needs **66** to command a majority, not the usual 61. On election night the right bloc of National, ACT and NZ First was projected at 67 seats, one above the line. The left bloc of Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity stood at 63.

## What are special votes?

**They are votes cast outside a voter's own electorate, by people on the unpublished roll, or by people who enrolled after October 4, the writ day.** Overseas votes make up less than a fifth of them. They are counted after election night, up to ten days after the poll, because each has to be checked.

They are not a small tail. In 2020 there were 603,257 special votes, just over 20% of the total, and a similar share is expected now. In 2023 they **cost National two seats and gave Te Pāti Māori two and the Greens one**, and in recent elections they have leaned to left-leaning parties. With National and Labour 0.1 points apart and some electorates decided by tiny margins, such as National's 69-vote lead in Wairarapa, a shift of that kind could change who leads.

## Why does the result take weeks?

**Because the count, the checking of special votes and the coalition talks run one after another.** The Electoral Commission's official result is due on **Friday, November 27**, 20 days after election day. Talks between parties can begin earlier, but with tight margins and a threshold party they often wait for the final numbers. In 2023 the talks took about 40 days, in 2020 about four weeks, and after the first MMP election in 1996 two months.

## What should readers watch next?

**Three numbers: the size of the House, National's total and Opportunity's decision.** If special votes trim National's overhang, the bar for a majority could drop. Prime Minister Luxon has ruled out working with Opportunity, which says it would approach National first only if its bottom lines are met, and Labour leader Hipkins has said he is open to talking to it. Until November 27 the government stays in a caretaker mode, in which it avoids major new decisions.

The cost of living drove the campaign: household costs rose 3% in the year to the September 2026 quarter, with petrol up 19.1%, and unemployment is at 5.7%, an 11-year high. Whoever forms the next government will inherit those numbers, but the first thing New Zealand will learn is simply how big its Parliament is.
