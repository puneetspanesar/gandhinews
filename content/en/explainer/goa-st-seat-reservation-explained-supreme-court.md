## Why does Goa have no tribal seats?

**Because the tribes were counted too late.** Goa has 40 assembly seats. One, Pernem, is reserved for Scheduled Castes. None is reserved for Scheduled Tribes, even though they make up about a tenth of the state.

The reason is timing. Under the Constitution, seats are reserved for STs in proportion to their share of a state's population, and the present map of constituencies across India was drawn on the basis of the 2001 Census. In 2001 Goa recorded only 566 tribal people out of about 13 lakh. Then in January 2003 the Centre **added the Gawda, Kunbi and Velip communities** to Goa's ST list.

By the 2011 Census, Goa's ST population stood at **1.49 lakh, or about 10.2%** of the state. But the Constitution freezes the readjustment of seats until the figures of the first Census after 2026 are published, so that count could not be used to reserve any seats.

## What did Parliament do about it?

**It passed a special law for Goa.** Tribal groups campaigned for more than two decades. The Goa assembly passed a resolution in July 2023, the Union Cabinet approved a Bill in March 2024, and after stalling in Lok Sabha in 2024 and again in mid-2025 it was finally passed by Parliament in August 2025. The Readjustment of Representation of Scheduled Tribes in Assembly Constituencies of the State of Goa Act came into force in August 2026.

The Act works in two steps. First, under Section 3(1), the Census Commissioner **estimates the ST share of Goa's population** and publishes it in the official gazette. Then the Election Commission amends the order that fixes Goa's constituencies to reserve seats in that proportion, after inviting public objections. The change would apply from the next assembly election, due in early 2027.

Politicians in Goa expect **about four seats** to be reserved, in areas with large tribal populations such as Sanguem, Quepem, Priol and Nuvem.

## What did the High Court stop?

**The first step.** A Goa resident, Edwin Lucio Fernandes, has challenged the law as unconstitutional. His petition argues that reserved seats cannot be reallocated until the first Census after 2026 is done, and that a special law cannot get around that. His lawyers also warned that once the population figure was notified, it "would attain finality and could not be questioned before any court".

On October 29 a bench of Justices Valmiki Menezes and Amit S Jamsandekar ordered that, until the petition is decided, the Census Commissioner **is restrained from notifying the estimate** in the Gazette of India.

## Why did the court doubt the estimate?

**Because the Census Commissioner's own letter admitted the data was thin.** In a communication to the Union Law Ministry on September 10, the Commissioner said the available information was insufficient to determine or estimate Goa's current population. Yet the same letter said the ST share could be "reasonably presumed" to be **about 9.9%**, working from the 2001 Census. The bench asked how that conclusion could be reached on the Commissioner's own admission.

## What is Goa doing now?

**Taking the fight to the Supreme Court.** A delegation including Speaker Ganesh Gaonkar and Art and Culture Minister Ramesh Tawadkar went to New Delhi on Monday. Narendra Sawaikar, Goa's NRI Commissioner, who was part of it, said the state would seek to have the stay lifted and would "file a Special Leave Petition (SLP) before the Supreme Court by Wednesday."

Chief Minister Pramod Sawant has said his government remains committed to reservation for tribal communities in the assembly and will also urge the Centre, which framed the law and appointed the Census Commissioner, to approach the court.

## Why does the timing matter so much?

**Because the election is only months away.** Goa's assembly polls are expected early in 2027. The Election Commission cannot redraw reserved seats until it has the Census Commissioner's figure, and it must then invite and hear objections. Every week of delay makes it harder to finish before the poll schedule is announced.

The new national Census will not help in time. Most of India is to be counted in February 2027, and its figures will take much longer to be published.

## What can the Supreme Court do?

**Lift the stay, keep it, or hear the whole case itself.** If the stay goes, the Commissioner can notify the estimate and the Election Commission can start work on four tribal seats. If it stays, Goa will most likely vote in 2027 on the old map, and its tribal communities will wait for the post-2026 Census and a fresh delimitation. Either way, the court will have to weigh two things the Constitution cares about: fair representation for groups left out by an accident of dates, and the rule that the count, not an estimate, decides who gets a reserved seat.
