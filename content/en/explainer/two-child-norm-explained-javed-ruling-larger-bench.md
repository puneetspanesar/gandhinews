## What is the two-child norm?

**It is a rule that bars people with more than two living children from standing in local elections.**

Several states wrote it into their panchayat laws in the 1990s, and some extended it to municipal bodies. A candidate who has a third child is not allowed to contest, and a sitting member who has one can be **disqualified from office**. Haryana's Panchayati Raj Act of 1994 had such a clause, and so did the panchayat law of undivided Andhra Pradesh, also from 1994. Maharashtra added it to its Village Panchayats Act in 2000.

The idea behind it was population control: those who lead villages and towns were expected to set an example on family size. Some states apply a similar test to government jobs. Rajasthan, for instance, bars candidates with more than two children from police constable posts.

## What did the Supreme Court decide in Javed?

**It upheld the rule as constitutional.** In Javed v State of Haryana, decided on 30 July 2003, a bench of three judges, Justices R C Lahoti, Ashok Bhan and Arun Kumar, heard challenges to Sections 175(1)(q) and 177(1) of the Haryana law.

The petitioners argued that the bar was arbitrary and broke the right to equality under Article 14, the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, and the freedom of religion under Article 25, since Muslim personal law allows a man more than one wife. The court rejected all three. It held that separating candidates with two children from those with more was a **reasonable classification** linked to the law's aim of controlling population.

The court also said the right to contest a panchayat election is not a fundamental right but a right given by statute, so the legislature can set conditions on it. On religion, it held that Article 25 protects only practices integral to a faith, and that having more than one wife was not one of them. Javed has since been followed, including in 2024 when another three-judge bench upheld Rajasthan's police recruitment rule.

## Which states still have it?

**Six, according to the court's amicus curiae.** At an August hearing, advocate Rukmini Bobde gave the bench a compilation of state laws. It showed that seven states have repealed their two-child norms and 15 never had one. The rule survives in **Assam, Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Odisha and Uttarakhand**.

The repeals have come recently and mostly in the south. Andhra Pradesh, worried about a falling birth rate, passed bills in November 2024 to drop the rule from its panchayat and municipal laws. Telangana followed in January 2026. In March this year Rajasthan's government moved a bill to end its own 30-year-old rule, citing a fall in the state's fertility rate.

## Why did the case come back now?

**Because of one sarpanch and a sharp fall in births.** Mangala Bhimrao Ingle, a former sarpanch of Kakoda gram panchayat in Maharashtra, was disqualified after her third child was born. The Bombay High Court upheld that in August 2025, and she appealed.

Justices PS Narasimha and Alok Aradhe used her case to question the rule itself. At a hearing in July they called it a "useless" policy. On Wednesday they said the country had changed since 2003: India's total fertility rate, then 3, is now **1.9, below the replacement rate of 2.1**, the level at which a population holds steady. "We are of the strong opinion that the laws which penalise having more than two children have lost their relevance," the bench said.

## What does a reference to a larger bench mean?

**That only a bigger bench can overturn Javed.** In the Supreme Court a smaller bench is bound by the ruling of a larger one. Javed was decided by three judges, so a bench of two cannot set it aside however strongly it disagrees. What it can do, and did, is record its doubts and send the question up.

The papers now go to the Chief Justice of India, who decides when a bench of more than three judges will be set up to hear it. That bench could overrule Javed, uphold it, or settle the matter on narrower grounds. The two judges said their view had **compelled them to stay the proceedings** in Ingle's case until then.

## What changes in the meantime?

**Nothing on the ground, for now.** Javed remains good law, and the two-child bar stays in force in the six states that still have it. Anyone with a third child in those states can still be kept out of a panchayat or municipal contest.

What has changed is the direction of travel. The country's top court has now said, in a pronounced judgment, that the rule no longer fits a country whose birth rate has dropped below replacement. That gives the remaining states a strong signal, and gives anyone disqualified under their laws a ground to go to court. No date has been set for the larger bench.
