Pax Silica sounds like a trade treaty, but it is closer to a club. On Sunday, November 15, **India and nine other governments signed** the San Francisco Declaration, a short text that commits them to protect the technologies and infrastructure that artificial intelligence runs on. This explainer sets out what the grouping is, what was signed and what it changes for India.

## What is Pax Silica?

**It is a US-led coalition to secure the supply chains behind AI.** Washington launched it in December 2025 around three things: semiconductors, critical minerals and the AI systems built on them. The aim is to reduce "coercive dependencies", a polite way of describing the hold China has over parts of that chain, rare earths above all.

The name plays on "silicon" and the old idea of a peace kept by a dominant power. Jacob Helberg, the US Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs, who runs it, has put it this way: **"Economic security is national security."**

India was invited in January and joined on **February 20, 2026**, signing the Pax Silica Declaration on the sidelines of the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi. Analysts noted that India was asked in even though it is not yet a major chip maker, because of its policy momentum and long-term potential.

## What was signed in San Francisco?

**A pledge to treat the security of sensitive technologies and critical infrastructure as a shared job.** The signatories are the United States, Australia, India, Israel, Japan, South Korea, Qatar, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom. South Korea announced its own signature on Tuesday.

The text names two kinds of asset. "Sensitive technologies" covers the goods, software, materials and know-how used in what it calls "super intelligence" supply chains, a phrase a footnote says some participants use to mean AI. Critical infrastructure covers ports, energy, communications and digital networks. Reports of the text say it calls for a **common framework for identifying such assets**, for diversified supply chains, and for tougher national action against economic coercion and undue foreign access or control.

It also proposes **five working groups**: innovation-friendly policy, adoption of the technology across sectors, supply chain cooperation, "safe harbors" and security hubs, and the security of AI systems themselves.

## Is it binding?

**No, and the signatories say so themselves.** South Korea's foreign ministry called it **"a non-binding political statement"** and noted that "no specific country is mentioned in the text". There are no penalties, no inspections and no shared fund. What it does is set a common baseline and a place to negotiate, with the working groups turning principles into rules later.

That is why a Korean analyst read it as a bargaining aid, meant to keep the American side of the rope taut in talks with China. The comment was translated from Korean.

## What does it mean for India?

**A seat at the table, and some questions about who India buys from.** India is trying to build chip assembly, testing and design at home under its Semiconductor Mission. Analysts expect cooperation to start with chip design, mature nodes of 28 nanometres and above, packaging and supply chain security rather than the most advanced fabrication. Membership could help draw allied investment and lower the political risk for firms building plants.

The working groups are where rules on trusted suppliers and security hubs will be drafted, and those rules could shape **which suppliers Indian firms can buy from** and who can invest in them. A pledge to guard against "undue foreign access or control" could, once turned into national rules, touch Indian companies' links with Chinese suppliers. Neither the text nor the reports say what India has committed to beyond the shared wording, and New Delhi had not issued its own statement at the time of writing.

## Why now?

**Because of the meeting in Shenzhen.** President Donald Trump is expected to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping there on Thursday, November 19, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, to discuss extending a trade truce that includes a pause on some export controls on critical minerals. China has also drawn up draft rules that would put 23 AI technologies on an export control list. A larger group signed up in San Francisco gives Washington more weight going in.

## What to watch next

Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are **expected to sign in the week of November 23**, according to reports, after worrying that the wording was too broad and cut across their own regulatory freedom. Not all of that is confirmed. The second thing to watch is the setting up of the working groups, and who from India sits on them. The third is whether any signatory is asked to change its rules on Chinese suppliers. Until one is, Pax Silica remains a statement of direction, and its weight will be measured in what the working groups produce.
