## What is the current account?

**It is the part of a country's accounts with the rest of the world that records what it earns and spends on everyday transactions.**

Every payment between India and other countries ends up in the balance of payments, which the Reserve Bank of India compiles. The current account is one half of it. It has four parts: trade in goods, trade in services, income such as interest, dividends and wages, and transfers, which in India's case mostly means money sent home by Indians working abroad.

If the total coming in is less than the total going out, India runs a current account deficit. If it is more, there is a surplus.

## Why does India usually run a deficit?

**Because it imports far more goods than it exports, and services and remittances only partly fill the gap.**

India buys large amounts of crude oil, gold, electronics and machinery from abroad. In April to June 2026 the goods trade deficit was $86.1 billion. Two strengths soften that blow. Exports of IT and business services left a net surplus of $51.6 billion, and remittances brought in $42.9 billion. On the other side, India paid out a net $10.5 billion in income, mainly interest and profits earned by foreign investors.

Put together, the quarter ended with a deficit of $4.2 billion, compared with $3.4 billion a year earlier.

## How is it measured?

**The RBI compiles it from customs data, bank reports of foreign exchange transactions and company surveys, and publishes it in dollars.**

Goods trade figures come largely from the commerce ministry's customs records. Services, income and transfers are built mainly from what banks report about foreign exchange payments and receipts, checked against surveys of companies. The RBI publishes full quarterly figures about two months after a quarter ends; the April to June data came out on 1 September. It also releases preliminary monthly figures, and the August numbers came out on 15 October.

Because a $5 billion gap means more for a small economy than a large one, the deficit is usually expressed as a share of GDP. India's April to June deficit was 0.5% of GDP.

## Who pays for the deficit?

**Foreign money does, through the other half of the balance of payments.**

A current account deficit has to be financed. The capital and financial account records how: foreign direct investment in factories and companies, portfolio investment in shares and bonds, loans, and deposits by non-resident Indians. If those inflows are larger than the deficit, the balance of payments shows a surplus and the RBI's reserves can rise. If they fall short, the RBI must draw on its reserves to fill the gap, or the rupee weakens.

That is what happened in April to June. Foreign portfolio investors pulled out a net $9.6 billion, and the reserves fell by $8.1 billion.

## Why does it matter for the rupee?

**Because a deficit means India needs more dollars than its trade brings in, and the rupee's price reflects that demand.**

Oil companies, importers and borrowers repaying foreign loans all need dollars. When the current account gap is wide and foreign investors are selling, demand for dollars outstrips supply and the rupee falls. A weaker rupee in turn makes imports, above all fuel, more expensive, which can feed into inflation.

This year the rupee has been under heavy pressure from crude prices above $100 a barrel and selling by foreign investors. It came close to 97 to the dollar in early October before recovering to 94.89 on Friday. The narrower August deficit is one reason the pressure has eased a little.

## How big is too big?

**There is no fixed line, but India has seen what a large deficit can do.**

In 2012-13 the deficit reached 4.8% of GDP. When the US Federal Reserve signalled in 2013 that it would slow its money printing, foreign investors pulled out of emerging markets and the rupee fell sharply. India was among the countries hit hardest. Today's deficit is a fraction of that, and the reserves are far larger.

What matters as much as the size is how it is financed. Long-term foreign direct investment is steadier than portfolio money, which can leave in days. A small deficit funded by volatile flows can still unsettle the rupee, as April to June showed.
