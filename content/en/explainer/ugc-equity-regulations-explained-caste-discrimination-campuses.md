## What are the UGC equity regulations?

**A set of rules telling every college and university how to prevent and deal with discrimination.** Their full name is the University Grants Commission (Promotion of Equity in Higher Education Institutions) Regulations, 2026. The UGC notified them on January 13, and they apply to **all higher education institutions** in the country, including deemed universities.

They replaced the UGC's 2012 equity rules. They grew out of a public interest case filed in 2019 by Radhika Vemula and Abeda Salim Tadvi, mothers of Rohith Vemula and Payal Tadvi, two students who died by suicide after alleged caste discrimination. That petition complained that the 2012 rules were not being enforced, and the Supreme Court **directed the UGC to frame new regulations**.

## What do they require colleges to do?

**Build a standing machinery for complaints.** Every institution must set up an **Equal Opportunity Centre** to run equity policies and guide students from disadvantaged groups. Small colleges can rely on the centre of the university they are affiliated to.

Through the centre works an **Equity Committee** chaired by the head of the institution, with seats for members of the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, persons with disabilities and women. Campuses must also run a 24-hour Equity Helpline, name Equity Ambassadors in departments and hostels, and deploy mobile Equity Squads to keep watch on places where students are vulnerable.

## How is a complaint handled?

**On a tight clock.** A complaint can be made online, in writing, by email or through the helpline, and the complainant's identity can be kept confidential. The Equity Committee must **meet within 24 hours** of a report and give its findings within 15 working days. The head of the institution then has seven working days to act. Cases that look criminal go straight to the police.

Institutions must also file reports twice a year on the make-up of their students, dropout rates and the status of complaints. A national monitoring committee of the UGC oversees the system.

## What happens to a college that does not comply?

**It can be cut off from the UGC.** Under Regulation 11, a defaulting institution can be **barred from UGC schemes**, stopped from offering degree, online or distance programmes, and removed from the UGC's list under Sections 2(f) and 12B of the UGC Act, 1956, which decides eligibility for central grants. Further penalties can be imposed depending on how serious the failure is.

## Why were the rules challenged?

**Over one definition.** Clause 3(c) defines **"caste-based discrimination"** as discrimination only against members of the SCs, STs and OBCs. A wider clause, 3(e), covers discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, gender, place of birth or disability.

Petitioners led by advocate Vishnu Shankar Jain argued that Clause 3(c) leaves general category students outside the caste protection and breaches the right to equality under Article 14. They also said the rules carry **no penalty for false complaints** and leave out ragging, and objected that the Equity Committee has no seat set aside for the unreserved category. The rules set off protests on campuses, with some general category groups seeking their withdrawal and some reserved category groups opposing it.

## What did the Supreme Court do?

**It put the rules on hold on January 29.** A bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justice Joymalya Bagchi found the regulations prima facie vague and open to misuse, and kept them in abeyance. Using its powers under Article 142, it ordered that **the 2012 regulations will continue** until further orders.

Justice Bagchi asked whether Clause 3(c) was needed at all, given the wider definition in Clause 3(e). The Chief Justice said his concern was an effective mechanism to protect marginalised students, and warned against any move towards separate hostels: "For gods sake, please don't do that." Senior advocate Indira Jaising, for the mothers, argued that the rules aim at an inclusive campus. The challenge was tagged with their 2019 case.

## What did the Centre say on November 5?

**That it may form a committee to review the rules.** In August Solicitor General Tushar Mehta had told the court the regulations were "under reconsideration". On Thursday the bench went further and asked: "Why don't you revisit the UGC Regulations?"

Mr Mehta replied that the government was examining the matter so that **no section feels left out**. "The government has taken a decision that every student should be taken care of," he said. The court deferred the hearing.


## What could change now?

**The definition is the likeliest target.** The bench's own questions centred on Clause 3(c), and petitioners want it made caste-neutral. In August the mothers' counsel asked the court to set a timeline for the government's decision. Until a new version is notified or the court rules, campuses work under **the 2012 rules**, as of November 2026.
