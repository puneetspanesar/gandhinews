## What did Albertans vote on?

**Whether to stay in Canada, or to start down the road to leaving it.**

The ballot asked: "Should Alberta remain a province of Canada or should the Government of Alberta commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether or not Alberta should separate from Canada?"

It was the first time in the province's history that the question of leaving Canada had been put directly to voters. The vote was nonbinding. A win for the second option would not have taken Alberta out of Canada; it would only have obliged the provincial government to begin preparing a second, binding vote.

Voters also received nine separate ballots on other questions put by Premier Danielle Smith's United Conservative Party government. Five deal with immigration and public services, including whether Alberta should take more control over immigration, whether newcomers without permanent status should wait 12 months before getting provincially funded social support, and whether they should pay a fee for health care and education. Four are constitutional, among them abolishing the Senate and letting provincial laws override conflicting federal ones.

## What was the result?

**A clear vote to stay.** Early results released late on Monday night, Alberta time, showed about 76% backing the option of remaining in Canada.

Interest was unusually high. More than 1.35 million votes were cast in advance polls and by mail before polling day, against roughly 810,000 in the 2023 provincial election. Elections Alberta received 519,082 mail-in ballots.

"They chose unity, to build together, and to take care of each other," Prime Minister Mark Carney said in a statement. Smith, who campaigned for the nine other questions rather than for separation, wrote: "Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada." The nine other questions are being counted one by one, with results expected by Wednesday.

## Why was the vote held at all?

**Because of a petition law the government had just made easier to use, and a long-running sense of grievance.**

In May 2025 Alberta amended its Citizen Initiative Act, cutting the number of signatures needed to force a referendum from 20% of eligible voters to 10% of the votes cast at the last election, and giving campaigners 120 days instead of 90 to collect them. Separatist and pro-Canada groups both launched petitions. The pro-Canada campaign led by Thomas Lukaszuk, a former deputy premier, gathered 404,293 verified signatures.

Neither petition's wording ended up on the ballot. Instead, in March 2026 the Smith government ordered a referendum with its own separation question alongside the nine others. Behind it lies years of anger in the oil-producing province at federal energy and environmental policies, which separatist groups have turned into a case for leaving.

## Can a Canadian province actually leave?

**Only through a long process, and never on its own.**

Canada's Constitution has no clause allowing a province to secede. In 1998, after Quebec's narrow 1995 referendum, the Supreme Court of Canada gave its opinion in the Secession Reference. It said a province had no right to leave unilaterally, but that a clear majority voting for secession on a clear question would oblige the rest of Canada to negotiate. Any deal would then need a constitutional amendment.

Parliament wrote those ideas into the Clarity Act in 2000. It lets the House of Commons decide, before and after a vote, whether a referendum question and the majority behind it are clear enough to start negotiations.

## What happens next?

**Probably not another referendum soon.** Smith has said a simple majority of 50% plus one would decide the matter and that there would be no follow-up vote on separation during her government's mandate.

The separatist movement says it is not finished. "Our movement isn't going anywhere," said Jeffrey Rath, a co-founder of the Alberta Prosperity Project, who is pushing for a special party meeting on whether Smith still has the confidence of the UCP. Opposition NDP leader Naheed Nenshi struck the opposite note: "We saved Canada. Now, we save Alberta."

The bigger political test may be the nine other questions. If Albertans back more provincial control over immigration and federal programmes, Smith will take those results to Ottawa as a mandate, setting up a fresh round of argument with Carney's government even after the question of leaving has been answered.
