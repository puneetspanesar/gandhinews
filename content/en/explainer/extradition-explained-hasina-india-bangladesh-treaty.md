## What is extradition?

**It is one country handing a wanted person to another, under a treaty and a court process.** The country asking sends a formal request. The country holding the person checks that the offence and the paperwork meet the treaty, and a court in that country examines the case before the government decides. A person cannot be simply deported to face trial: extradition has legal grounds and legal exits.

India and Bangladesh signed an extradition treaty in January 2013, and it came into force later that year. It was amended in 2016.

## Why is it in the news now?

**Because Dhaka has told Sheikh Hasina that extradition is her only way home.** On Monday, November 9, lawyers for the former prime minister wrote to the Bangladesh High Commission in New Delhi asking for a new passport and national ID card. Dhaka refused. Ministers said she must be extradited from India, surrender to a court and serve her sentence.

Hasina has been in India since the uprising of August 2024. In November 2025 a tribunal in Dhaka sentenced her to death in absentia for crimes against humanity over the 2024 crackdown. She denies the charge. Dhaka first asked India for her extradition in December 2024 and renewed the request after the verdict.

## What does the treaty say?

**It covers serious crimes, and it was made easier to use in 2016.** The treaty defines an extraditable offence as conduct punishable in both countries with at least **one year's imprisonment**. The offence need not have the same name in both. Helping, abetting and attempting are covered.

The 2016 amendment changed Article 10(3). A country asking for an accused person no longer has to supply evidence of the offence. According to reports, **a competent court's arrest warrant and proof of identity are enough**. For someone already convicted, such as Hasina, there is a further rule that the sentence must be at least four months.

## Where can India say no?

**In three places, and each needs a reason the treaty recognises.**

First, political offences. Article 6 lets a country refuse for an offence "of a political character". But the same article says murder, culpable homicide, kidnapping, hostage-taking, incitement to murder and certain terrorism and explosives offences are not political. Being a political figure is not enough. Dhaka argues that crimes against humanity involving killings cannot be treated as political.

Second, good faith. The treaty allows refusal if the accusation is not made in good faith in the interests of justice, or if surrender would be unjust or oppressive. This is the ground on which Hasina's side is most likely to argue, since she calls the trial politically driven.

Third, India's own law. Under the Extradition Act the Centre can stop proceedings and discharge a fugitive where a request is politically motivated or surrender would be unjust or inexpedient, and it cannot surrender someone for a political offence.

## What about the death sentence?

**The treaty does not make it an automatic bar.** Capital punishment is not listed as a ground for refusal, and Bangladesh still has the death penalty. It could still feed the argument that surrender would be unjust or oppressive. India's Act provides for life imprisonment instead of death where the requesting country does not have the penalty for the offence, but that does not apply here.


## What has India said?

**Only that it is examining the requests.** The foreign ministry has said the matter is being considered under applicable law and procedures, and has not said how it will answer. Analysts quoted in recent reports expect India to keep sheltering her without helping her return or restoring her political standing.

This is where the law ends and politics begins. The treaty gives India grounds to refuse if it wishes and, on the better reading, little ground to refuse **merely because she is a political figure**. Which route New Delhi takes will be shaped by its wish for a working relationship with the elected government in Dhaka, which took power after February's election, and by its history with Hasina. That judgement is ours, not a legal finding.


## What happens next

If India decided to act on a request, a court in India would examine it before the Centre made the final call, so a handover would not be quick. For now Hasina says she will return in December, while Dhaka says she may come only as an extradited convict. **No date has been set** for any Indian decision, and the foreign ministry in Dhaka is still examining the lawyers' email.
