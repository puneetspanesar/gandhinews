## What is the Forest Rights Act?

**It is a 2006 law that recognises the rights of people who have lived in forests for generations, many of them never recorded in government papers.**

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 was passed to correct what the law itself calls a "historical injustice": forest land was taken into state records without settling the rights of the people already living on it. The Act turns those claims into legal rights that a title can record.

## What rights does it give?

**Rights to land, produce and community resources.** Individual rights cover holding and living on forest land for habitation or self-cultivation, up to four hectares. Community rights cover grazing, fishing, water bodies and the collection, use and sale of **minor forest produce**, a term the Act defines as produce of plant origin that occurs naturally in the forest, such as bamboo, tendu leaves or honey. Communities can also claim the right to protect and manage their forest.

## Who counts as a forest dweller?

**Someone who actually lives in the forest and depends on it.** The Act names two groups. A Forest Dwelling Scheduled Tribe must primarily reside in forests and depend on them for a livelihood. An Other Traditional Forest Dweller must show **three generations, or 75 years, of residence** in the area before December 13, 2005. Owning a plot, or cultivating one from outside, is not the test.

## How does a claim travel?

**It starts with the village.** A gram sabha, the village assembly, receives and verifies the claim and passes a resolution. The claim then goes to a sub-divisional level committee, and finally to a district level committee, which takes the decision. The process matters because the court found Tamil Nadu primarily responsible for delay in forming the gram sabhas at Megamalai, where about 40 had been formed and about 1,600 claims received.

## What happened at Megamalai?

**Thousands of people farming silk cotton inside a tiger reserve asked to be treated as forest dwellers.** The Megamalai forests are part of the Srivilliputhur-Megamalai Tiger Reserve in Tamil Nadu. The state's affidavit of September 6, 2026, listed **4,594 occupants on 5,072.64 hectares**, of which 3,637.83 hectares are under silk cotton. It sorted them into three groups: 1,595 permanent residents cultivating inside the forest, 232 temporary residents and 2,767 people living outside it.

The case sits inside a larger one. On May 29, 2026, the court had ordered time-bound eviction, rehabilitation and ecological restoration of the Agasthyamalai landscape.

## What did the court decide?

**That a planted commercial crop is not minor forest produce.** Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta held that the idea of planned cultivation of a commercial crop is "totally contrary to" the concept of minor forest produce in Section 3(1)(c), and that the state's own affidavit described silk cotton as a plantation introduced from the 1930s. Planned or promoted cultivation of a commercial crop in forest areas is, the bench said, **"completely impermissible"**, and the critical tiger habitat must be given primacy over claims that encroach on it.

It excluded the second and third groups, a total of **2,999 people**, from consideration under the Act, saying they "cannot, on the basis of such cultivation alone, qualify". The first group is not excluded.

## Who looks at the rest?

**A Special Committee headed by former Chief Justice of India B R Gavai.** It will scrutinise the decisions on the remaining claims and report in a sealed cover, with monthly progress reports from the state.

The court also asked the Central Empowered Committee for its opinion on whether silk cotton can be classed as minor forest produce at all, and doubted the state's statement that no commercial enterprise financed the farming, given 2,767 cultivators living outside the forest.

## What does it mean beyond Megamalai?

**It draws a line on where the Act's protection ends.** The Act was written for people who live in and draw their living from forests. The order says residence and dependence decide who is a forest dweller. States and forest officials in other tiger reserves now have a Supreme Court reading to cite when non-resident cultivators claim an Act right. It does not remove the rights of those who do qualify: the permanent residents of Megamalai keep their claims, on a fixed timetable.

## What happens next?

Tamil Nadu has 30 days from the order to form the remaining gram sabhas, after which claims must be decided within five months. The matter returns to the court on **February 1, 2027** for an interim status report.
