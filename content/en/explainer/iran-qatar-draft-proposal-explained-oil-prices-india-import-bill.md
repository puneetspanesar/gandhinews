## What are Iran and Qatar talking about?

**A way to end the war between Iran and the United States, and to reopen the Strait of Hormuz.**

The war began in late February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran. Ceasefires agreed in April and June collapsed within weeks. Since then Iran has squeezed shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel that carries a large share of the world's oil out of the Gulf, while the United States has blockaded Iranian ports. Qatar, along with Pakistan, has been carrying messages between the two sides, since Tehran refuses to meet American officials directly for now.

## What is on the table?

**Two plans, which overlap but disagree on what comes first.**

In late September Iran offered a seven-day plan: an end to fighting in Iran and Lebanon, the release of billions of dollars of frozen Iranian assets, the lifting of US sanctions on Iranian oil and an end to the blockade. In return Iran would let the strait reopen, and both sides would resume talks on its nuclear programme. President Donald Trump rejected it on 26 September.

On 25 October Qatari mediators took **a new draft to Tehran**. It also runs on a seven-day timetable, with each side taking a confidence-building step every day and several countries acting as guarantors. A senior Iranian official dismissed it as "unacceptable ideas and not an offer". The sticking point is order: Iran wants the blockade and oil sanctions gone first, while Washington wants **Iran's nuclear programme on the table from the start**.

## What changed in the past few days?

**The tone.** The Qatari team went back to Tehran on 30 October, in coordination with Washington. Over the weekend Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran could **reopen the strait within two days** once the US accepted its conditions, and Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei said a letter from US Vice President JD Vance showed Washington's "tone has changed completely". On Sunday Araghchi said the talks were "ongoing and progressing".

On Monday Iranian negotiators left their latest round with the Qatari mediators with **a draft proposal said to be circulating** and a follow-up meeting possible, according to an official briefed on the talks. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump would like to end the conflict "diplomatically".

## Why did oil fall so sharply?

**Because traders began to price in peace.** Brent crude was down 4.2% at $108.64 a barrel early on Monday evening, India time, and **more than 6% at around $105** a little later. US crude slipped below $97. Only ten days earlier Brent had climbed above $117, its highest since March, after starting October below $97.

Supply has also held up better than feared. Tanker loadings at Fujairah and Omani terminals outside the strait are running 36% above pre-war levels, according to the ship-tracking firm Kpler, and US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said transit levels are back to normal "minus Iranian barrels". A deal would add those barrels back and remove the risk premium that every tanker attack adds to the price.

## What would a deal mean for India?

**A big saving on the import bill.** India buys about 88% of the crude it uses from abroad, and the war has made that oil far dearer. In April to September the country paid **$90.6 billion for crude imports**, 46% more than a year earlier, though it bought almost the same quantity. In September the Indian basket, the average price its refiners pay, was $116 a barrel against under $70 a year before.

At India's current rate of buying, about 245 million tonnes a year, every $10 off the barrel works out to **roughly $18 billion a year**. That would ease pressure on the rupee and the trade deficit, give oil companies room on petrol and diesel, and help airlines, whose fuel bills have soared.

## Is cheap oil now assured?

**No.** Nothing has been signed, and Iran has rejected drafts before. Trump had promised no strikes before Tuesday's US midterm elections, and US officials have warned that the weeks after the vote could be the most dangerous. Even with a deal, the world has drawn **nearly 1 billion barrels from its stockpiles** during the war, according to the head of the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, and they will need refilling. Goldman Sachs warned on Sunday that Brent could reach $140 in 2027 if Gulf output stays well below last year's level. For Indian buyers, the next few days of talks in Doha and Tehran matter as much as anything the oil ministry can do.
