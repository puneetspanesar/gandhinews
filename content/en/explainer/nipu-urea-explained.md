## What is urea, and why does India need so much?

**Urea is the cheapest and most widely used nitrogen fertiliser in India**, and farmers lean on it for wheat, rice and almost every other crop. It is made mainly from natural gas, and its price to the farmer is held down by the Centre, which pays the difference to the makers and importers as a subsidy.

India uses about **400 lakh tonnes of urea a year**. Its plants made 293.30 lakh tonnes in 2025-26, and the capacity the Centre now reckons for 2026-27 is 269.42 lakh tonnes a year. The gap of about 107 lakh tonnes between demand and last year's output is met from abroad.

## What did the Centre approve on November 25?

**Six new brownfield plants, each of 12.7 lakh tonnes a year and about ₹9,000 crore, so about ₹54,000 crore in all.** "Brownfield" means built on an existing fertiliser site, where land, utilities, logistics and trained staff are already in place. The Department of Fertilizers puts the saving from reusing that infrastructure at about ₹225 crore a year.

The six are MFL Madras in Chennai, NFL Nangal in Punjab, KRIBHCO's Shahjahanpur plant in Uttar Pradesh, Matix at Panagarh in West Bengal, CFCL at Gadepan near Kota in Rajasthan and IIPL at Jagdishpur in Uttar Pradesh. Two are public sector, one is a cooperative and three are private.

Together they add **76.2 lakh tonnes on paper**, about seven-tenths of last year's gap. In net terms the gain is smaller, because the Madras and Nangal projects replace old plants. The Madras plant is more than 60 years old, and the Nangal unit is about 50 to 55 years old with a capacity of 4.73 lakh tonnes a year.

## Why replace old plants at all?

**Because an old plant burns more gas for every tonne.** The release says Nangal uses 6.498 gigacalories of energy per tonne of urea, against an industry weighted average of 5.95 and a new norm of 5. Since gas is the main raw material, each extra gigacalorie is paid for in fuel, and the subsidy carries the cost.

## What does NIPU 2026 offer investors?

**A return on equity of 12% to 16%, and protection from exchange rates.** The 12% is the floor and the 16% the ceiling. Exchange rates will be normalised, and after four years the amounts will be converted into rupees at the rates then prevailing. A urea plant costs thousands of crores and its price is set by the government, so firms want that certainty before they commit.

NIPU is the successor to the New Investment Policy of 2012, announced on January 2, 2013, and amended on October 7, 2014. That policy produced six plants of the same 12.7 lakh tonne size: Ramagundam in Telangana, Gorakhpur, Sindri and Barauni through public sector joint ventures, and Matix's Panagarh and Chambal's Gadepan III in the private sector. They lifted capacity from 207.54 lakh tonnes in 2014-15 to 269.42 lakh tonnes in 2026-27.

## Why does the import bill matter?

**Imports have been large and costly.** The Fertiliser Association of India said on November 23 that India imported a record **about 10.35 million tonnes** of urea in 2025-26, and that imports in April to October 2026 were 7.20 million tonnes, 23% more than the 5.86 million tonnes a year earlier. Global prices swung from about $947 a tonne in May, after the West Asia war, to about $362 in October.

The Budget for 2026-27 sets aside about **₹1.17 lakh crore** for the urea subsidy, ₹91,000 crore for home-made urea and ₹32,000 crore for imports. A year earlier the import subsidy was planned at ₹21,000 crore and ended at ₹52,000 crore. The association expects the overall fertiliser subsidy to reach about ₹2.5 lakh crore against ₹1.71 lakh crore Budgeted. The exposure is not only to price: a UN warning that fertiliser supply through Hormuz was at risk showed how much depends on ships arriving.

## Will the new plants lower the cost?

**Not by themselves.** The association's figures show home-made urea can cost up to ₹66,000 a tonne at present against about ₹35,000 for imports, because of gas prices. What new plants change is the volume that need not be bought abroad, and the energy used per tonne, which the new norm of 5 gigacalories is meant to cut. For how the subsidy is set, see our explainer on the [nutrient based subsidy](nbs-explained-fertiliser-subsidy-rabi-2026-27-dap-urea).

## What happens next?

The release gives **no completion dates**, no bidding schedule and no financing breakdown for each site, and says more projects will follow. Two other plants of 12.7 lakh tonnes are already under way: Talcher Fertilizers in Odisha, which uses coal gasification and is due by December 2027, and the Namrup project in Assam. Imports will stay necessary until then, and even with every plant built the release expects a marked fall in import dependence, not an end to it. The things to watch are the final terms signed with each company, the start of construction, and whether the pipeline keeps to time.
