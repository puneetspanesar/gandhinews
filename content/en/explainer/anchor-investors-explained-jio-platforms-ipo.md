## What is an anchor investor?

**A large institution that agrees to buy a big block of an IPO before the public gets a chance.**

When a company sells shares to the public for the first time, the offer is divided into portions: one for qualified institutional buyers, such as mutual funds, banks, insurers and foreign funds, one for wealthy individuals and companies, and one for retail investors. Anchors come out of the institutional portion. They bid on a single day, one working day before the issue opens, and the company and its bankers decide who gets how much.

The idea, borrowed from markets abroad, is simple. If well-known funds are willing to commit serious money at a stated price, other investors have a reason to take the offer seriously. The anchor book is, in effect, a public vote of confidence.

## Who can be one?

**Only institutions, and only for large sums.** Mutual funds, insurers, pension funds, banks and foreign portfolio investors qualify. Individuals, including the very rich, do not. In a main-board IPO each anchor must apply for at least ₹10 crore.

## How much of an IPO can anchors take?

**Up to 60% of the institutional portion.** In a typical large issue that works out to a little under a third of all the shares on offer. At Jio Platforms the anchors took ₹8,110 crore of a ₹30,213 crore issue, roughly a quarter.

SEBI has also decided who inside that anchor book gets first claim. Since November 30, 2025, 40% of the anchor portion is set aside for Indian long-term money: 33% for domestic mutual funds and 7% for insurers and pension funds. If insurers and pension funds do not take up their share, it goes to mutual funds. The regulator said the change was meant to "broaden the participation of long-term institutional investors in the IPOs". The same reform allowed between five and 15 anchors for allocations of up to ₹250 crore, each getting at least ₹5 crore.

That is why the Life Insurance Corporation of India, SBI Mutual Fund and HDFC Mutual Fund sit alongside Capital Group, BlackRock and Singapore's government on Jio's list.

## Can anchors sell straight away?

**No.** Half the shares an anchor is allotted are locked in for 30 days from allotment and the other half for 90 days. The rule exists so that anchors cannot lend their names to an issue, collect the listing-day gain and leave. It also means two dates worth watching after any big listing: when the first and second lots of anchor shares are free to be sold, extra supply can weigh on the price.

## What if the final price is different?

**Anchors cannot pay less than the public.** If the issue price is finally set above the anchor price, anchors must pay the difference. If it is set lower, they get nothing back. Jio's anchors paid ₹1,119, the top of the ₹1,065 to ₹1,119 band, so the question does not arise.

## What does Jio's anchor book tell a retail investor?

**That institutions want the shares, though perhaps less than the headline suggests.** Bankers said the anchor portion drew bids of more than ₹2.5 lakh crore, over 30 times what was available, and described it as a record for India. But those were indicative bids, not money transferred. "Anchor demand of ₹2.5 trillion against an ₹8,110 crore book reflects indicative bids, not committed capital," an analyst at Samco Securities cautioned.

A strong anchor book has preceded weak listings before, and a modest one has preceded strong ones. It says that professionals are prepared to pay the asking price; it does not say the price is cheap. Retail investors bidding from October 21 until Friday, October 23, still have to make up their own minds about the business, the valuation of about ₹10.3 lakh crore at the top of the band and the ₹27,500 crore of debt the money will help repay. One small detail may be the more telling signal: the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, which has held a stake since 2020, was the only existing shareholder to ask for more.
