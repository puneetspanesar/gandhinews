On Friday afternoon a minibus carrying about 45 people, many of them college students, swerved into the wrong lane of National Highway 715 at Rangajan, near Kaziranga, and hit a container truck head-on. **Twenty-six people died.** The driver was among them.

The cause is still under inquiry, but one detail from officials deserves more attention than it has had. The minibus had been **fined in Jorhat in March for doing 102 km/h** and carried several overspeeding fines from the Kaziranga stretch, some of them still unpaid. A machine had seen this vehicle break the speed limit more than once. Nothing stopped it from carrying passengers on the same road.

## What the Court has already ordered

This is not a gap in the law. On September 28, a Supreme Court bench of Justices J B Pardiwala and K V Viswanathan, hearing the long-running road safety case S. Rajaseekaran v. Union of India, noted that about **₹49,194 crore in e-challan fines was recoverable and ₹26,175 crore recovered**. Subtracting one from the other leaves roughly ₹23,000 crore unpaid.

The order says authorities shall bar a vehicle with pending fines from renewing its registration certificate, deny it a fitness certificate, stop pollution-check centres from issuing it a certificate, blacklist it on the Parivahan portal and block its transfer. For multiple violations the driving licence is to be suspended from renewal. And "let there be random checking of the vehicles", with "necessary steps" to **impound the vehicle itself** where the challan has not been honoured.

## Why the order may not be enough

An order of this kind lives or dies in the weeks after it is passed. Freezing a registration renewal does nothing to a minibus whose papers are valid for another year. Fitness is checked at fixed intervals. A fine that shows up only when the owner next needs a service is a fine the owner can wait out. The Court set no deadline for these steps. It asked the road transport ministry to file an affidavit with a timeline, and the matter returns on November 25.

Speed is also the single largest killer on the road. The ministry's report for 2023, as reported, counted **1,72,890 deaths, of which 68.1% were attributed to over-speeding**, and 63,112 deaths on national highways. A vehicle with a record of speeding is exactly the one the system should be built to find.

## The fair objection

Owners will say that challans are issued by cameras without a hearing, that some are wrong, and that small operators cannot be treated as criminals over an unpaid fine. That is fair, and it is why the answer is not blind impounding. A challan should be contestable on a simple online process, with a time limit for the state to decide. But a vehicle that has been caught for speeding repeatedly, on a hill-and-forest road where the speed limit exists to protect animals and people, is not a close case.

## What states should do now

First, Assam, and every state, should **publish each month the number of vehicles stopped, impounded or denied services** under the September 28 order, district by district. The Court has asked the ministry for a public dashboard on electronic enforcement; states should not wait for it.

Second, repeat speeding by a passenger vehicle should trigger a **check on its permit and driver** within days, not at the next renewal. Under the amended Rule 21(25) cited by the Court, five or more offences in a year from January 1, 2026 already mark a driver as a danger to the public.

Third, the Golaghat inquiry should state in public how many challans the minibus had, what they were for, when they were issued and why nothing followed. If the answer is that the system could not act, that is the finding that matters.

Fourth, the speed limits near Kaziranga, set to protect wildlife crossing the highway, should be backed by cameras that feed the same enforcement system, so that a fine there carries the same consequence as a fine anywhere else.

The families of the 26 will receive ₹2 lakh each from the Prime Minister's relief fund. What they are owed more is an explanation of why a vehicle that had been caught was still on the road, and an assurance that the order the Court gave in September means something by December.
