Since Monday, delegations of the Karnataka Rakshana Vedike have been walking into Bengaluru's technology offices, from Bagmane Tech Park in C V Raman Nagar to Hewlett Packard Enterprise's campus near KR Puram, with memorandums and a pointed question: **how many of your staff are Kannadigas?** They want each firm's total workforce, its share of employees from Karnataka, three years of recruitment figures and its hiring criteria.

D K Shivakumar had asked them not to. "I appeal to all organisations not to visit IT-BT companies directly or take matters into their own hands," he said, adding that such issues "must be resolved through dialogue and within the framework of the law". The Vedike **rejected the appeal and carried on** into Tuesday.

## A real grievance

It would be wrong to wave the campaign away. Bengaluru has grown into one of the world's great technology cities on Karnataka's land, water, roads and universities. Young people in Mysuru, Kalaburagi or Hubballi can reasonably ask why so few of the jobs seem to reach them. A city's prosperity that its own state's graduates feel shut out of is a political problem, and the Vedike has given it a voice.

The Vedike also says it is gathering information rather than targeting any firm, and its demands include **more skill training and internships** for local youth. Those are sensible asks.

## Wrong door, wrong method

But a private company's reception desk is not the place to settle them. Activists entering offices to demand staff data, however politely, put employees and security guards in an awkward position and send a message to investors that no brochure can undo. Firms are not obliged to hand a private group their recruitment records, and they should not be pressed to.

The state has already been down the harder road. In July 2024 the Cabinet approved a Bill reserving **50% of management and 75% of non-management jobs** in private firms for locals, defined as people born in Karnataka, living there for 15 years and able to read and write Kannada. Within days it was put on hold. Nasscom warned that such rules "could force companies to relocate as local skilled talent becomes scarce", and said the tech sector made up a quarter of the state's economy.

Quotas of that kind are blunt. They tie hiring to birthplace rather than ability, invite fake domicile papers and would be hard to defend in court. The Vedike's campaign is, in part, the result of a promise made and then shelved.

## What the government should do

First, Karnataka should collect and publish **hiring data for the whole sector**, through its labour and IT departments, so that the debate rests on figures rather than impressions. If the share of local hires is low, the state will know where; if it is not, that too is worth knowing.

Second, it should put money into what firms actually hire for: engineering colleges outside Bengaluru that teach current skills, paid internships tied to companies in the city, and Kannada-medium students helped with English and technical training rather than left behind.

Third, it should work with industry on voluntary targets for local hiring and apprenticeships, reported every year, instead of reviving quotas that will be fought in court.

And it should make plain, through the police if needed, that **no group may walk into private offices** to demand records. Shivakumar's appeal was right. An appeal that is ignored needs to be followed by something firmer.

Kannadigas have every right to want a larger share of their capital's jobs. The way to get it runs through classrooms and honest numbers, not office lobbies.
