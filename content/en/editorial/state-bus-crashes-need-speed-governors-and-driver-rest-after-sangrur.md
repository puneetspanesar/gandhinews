At about 4:30 on Wednesday morning, a bus of the state-owned Pepsu Road Transport Corporation left the road at Sangrur, struck a pole under the flyover wall and then a tree. **Three passengers were killed** and more than 20 were hurt. It was not fog. A police officer at the scene said the weather was clear. Early accounts from police and passengers say the bus was **moving at high speed** and the driver lost control.

The inquiry will say more, and we should wait for it. But the shape of the event is familiar, and so is what follows: a condolence message, free treatment for the injured, a promise to look into it. The Chief Minister's office did not announce any payment for the families of the dead, though after another Sangrur crash on October 18 it announced **₹2 lakh for each family**. The sum a family receives should not depend on the day.

## The passenger has no say

A person who takes a private car chooses the driver. A person who boards a state bus does not. She trusts that the corporation has checked the vehicle, limited its speed and made sure the man at the wheel is rested. That trust is the whole contract of public transport. When a bus crashes on an empty road at 4:30 in the morning, the first questions are about the corporation: how many hours had the driver been on duty, how fast could the bus go, and who was watching.

At present, nobody outside the corporation can answer those questions. That is the failure, whatever the inquiry finds.

## The fair objection

State transport undertakings run thin. Crews are short, routes are long, and early departures are what commuters ask for. Blaming a driver after the fact is easy, and a single crash does not prove a systemic fault. The police have not named a cause.

All true. It is also why the answer cannot be left to one inquiry. If the corporation's records are clean, publishing them costs nothing and shows the public that a crash was an exception. If they are not, the public should know before the next early run, not after.

## What the state should do

First, **publish the inquiry result** within a fixed period, including the driver's duty hours in the days before the crash, the bus's speed record if it carries a tracker, and its last fitness check.

Second, every state transport corporation should publish, once a year, **fleet-wide data**: how many buses carry speed-limiting or tracking devices, how many drivers worked beyond the permitted hours, and how many crashes involved corporation buses.

Third, depots should set **minimum rest periods** between duties, and early-morning departures should not be staffed by crews who finished a late shift. Where there are too few drivers, the corporation should say so in its budget request instead of covering the gap with overtime.

Fourth, the **compensation rule** should be fixed in advance. A set sum for a death and a set sum for serious injury, paid within days, announced by the corporation rather than negotiated in the news.

Fifth, passengers should have a number to call or a message to send when a driver is speeding, and a log showing what became of each complaint.

None of this is expensive next to what a state spends on flyovers and highways. A road can be perfect and still kill, if the vehicle on it is not controlled. Three people got on a bus before dawn on Wednesday expecting to reach Chandigarh. The state that sold them the ticket should be able to show them, and their families, what it did to keep them safe.
