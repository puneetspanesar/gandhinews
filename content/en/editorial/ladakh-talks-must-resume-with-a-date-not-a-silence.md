Sonam Wangchuk had booked his ticket. On Thursday, the day the Home Ministry had indicated for the next round of talks on Ladakh's future, he said in a video message that the meeting was not happening. No new date has been announced. "They are moving away from every promise," he said.

A negotiation that stops without explanation is worse than one that fails in the open. It invites each side to assume the worst of the other, and in Ladakh there is already plenty of that.

## How the talks stalled

What happened over the past week is not in dispute. On October 16 the ministry handed the Leh Apex Body and the Kargil Democratic Alliance a 32-page framework. It proposed a new Article 371-K for Ladakh, followed by a regulation under Article 240, with a directly elected body for the Union Territory and safeguards on land, resources and culture. The two groups rejected it the same day. They said it went back on an in-principle understanding reached in May, on safeguards along the lines of those that Articles 371A, 371F and 371G give Nagaland, Sikkim and Mizoram.

Ladakh's Chief Secretary, Ashish Kundra, called the rejection unfortunate. "It was surprising to see that the document was dismissed outright within just two hours of being received," he said, adding that much in the draft was open for discussion. "Negotiations require an open mind, mutual trust and, above all, patience."

## The Centre's case

Kundra has a point, and it should be stated fairly. A 32-page constitutional text deserves more than two hours. The draft left many questions open, from how members would be elected to how the new body would sit alongside the hill councils and panchayats, and those are exactly the questions a negotiation is for. The Union Territory administration says the government is committed to a political solution. A Centre that has put a constitutional amendment on the table is not refusing to talk.

But patience has to run both ways. If the government wants Leh and Kargil to treat its draft as a starting point, it cannot let the next meeting simply lapse. Silence after a rejection reads, in Leh, as a decision. And the Ladakhi leaders' complaint is specific: that the text did not reflect what both sides had agreed months ago. That is a claim the ministry can answer, by showing in writing where the draft meets the May understanding and where it does not.

## What should happen now

First, the Home Ministry should announce a new date for talks within days, and say publicly why the October 22 round did not take place. A border region of strategic weight should not learn about the state of its own negotiations from a video on social media.

Second, both sides should put their positions on paper. The Leh and Kargil groups should set out, clause by clause, what they want changed in the draft. The ministry should publish the May understanding, or an agreed summary of it, so that each side can be held to what it said.

Third, the next round should begin with the questions Kundra himself listed: the composition and powers of the elected body, delimitation, and its relationship with the hill councils. Those are where the substance lies, and where an agreement is possible.

Ladakh's leaders have kept coming back to the table for years. The surest way to keep them there is to show that patience produces results. A date in the diary would be a start.
