A bail order is a court's finding that a person need not be in jail while his trial goes on. In Uttar Pradesh, for hundreds of people, that finding has stayed on paper.

When the state looked at just 20 of its 75 districts, it found **264 under-trial prisoners who had been granted bail** and were still behind bars, most of them because they could not furnish sureties or meet other conditions the court had set. It told the Supreme Court on Friday that 158 had since walked out. The other 106 are still waiting for a decision. Nobody yet knows the count for the remaining 55 districts.

The bench of Justices M M Sundresh and Prasanna B Varale has now extended its directions to the whole state, and put the burden where it belongs. "The onus lies on the state to make sure that these persons are released from custody," it said. It also told trial courts to change bail conditions where needed and ruled that a prisoner with a private lawyer is no less entitled to legal aid: "There cannot be any differentiation between the under-trial prisoners."

## Not a paperwork glitch

It is tempting to call this an administrative lag. It is more serious than that. Every day a bailed person spends in a cell is a day of detention that **no court has authorised**. The Constitution promises that no one will lose their liberty except by a procedure established by law; here the procedure has already said yes.

The same week brought a sharper case. On November 3, a bench of Justices J B Pardiwala and Manmohan found that a man named Saddam, from Baghpat, was **still in jail weeks after the Supreme Court itself had granted him bail** on September 28. His bail plea had earlier been adjourned 12 times in the Allahabad High Court. The judges called it "something very disturbing" and asked the state to show why it should not pay him compensation.

If an order of the country's highest court can sit unexecuted for weeks, the odds for a daily wage earner bailed by a magistrate in a district town are worse.

## The real reason is poverty

The fair objection is that sureties exist for a reason: they give the court some assurance that the accused will return. That is true. But a surety a prisoner cannot raise does not secure his attendance; it simply **turns bail into a fee** that only some can pay. The court's own direction to modify conditions recognises this.

The scale makes it urgent. About **73% of India's prisoners were under-trials** at the end of 2024, in jails holding 5.11 lakh people against room for 4.53 lakh. The law's newer tools, such as Section 479 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita for release after part of the maximum sentence, are still little used.

## What the state should do

First, every bail order should start a clock. If the person is not out within seven days, the jail superintendent should report the reason to the District Legal Services Authority and to the court that granted bail, in writing.

Second, legal aid lawyers should visit every jail each week with a list of bailed prisoners still inside, and move the court to **cut sureties to what the prisoner can actually pay**, or replace them with a personal bond.

Third, Uttar Pradesh should publish the district-wise count it gives the Supreme Court, with the number released each month, so that the progress the state has promised by January 20, 2027, can be seen by everyone.

Finally, where a person has been held after bail without good reason, compensation should follow as a matter of course, not as an exception. A state that has to pay for illegal detention will soon find it can make the paperwork move.

The court has done its part. The cell doors are the government's job.
