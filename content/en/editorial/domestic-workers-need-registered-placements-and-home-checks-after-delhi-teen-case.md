A 16-year-old girl was freed from a house in Abul Fazal Enclave-II in southeast Delhi on November 3 and 4. She has told the Child Welfare Committee that she was **burned with a hot iron** on her back, hands and hips, wounded with a heated knife, and kept without food and water for three days. She says she worked from 6 am to 2 am. Shaheen Bagh police have registered a case. As of Saturday, no one had been arrested.

Those are allegations, and the courts will test them. What is not in dispute is how she came to be there and how long it took anyone to notice.

## A year without a single visit

The Association for Voluntary Action says another woman placed the girl in the household and that she was paid **only ₹18,000 for nearly a year** of work. Her parents spoke to her on the phone two or three times in that period, the NGO says, and her face was covered during those calls. She was found only because an NGO complained to the police, and the sub-divisional magistrate then sent the Labour Department, police and NGO workers to the door.

That is the system working at its best, and it took a year. Nothing in the ordinary course of things would have brought an official to that house. Nothing reported suggests the girl had a contract, a record with any authority, or anyone outside the household obliged to look at her.

## The fair objection

Households will say that most domestic workers are treated decently, and that the state cannot send inspectors into every kitchen. Both are true. Privacy matters, and a rule that treats every employer as a suspect would be resented and ignored. Many poor families also rely on the wages a daughter sends home.

But the answer to a hard-to-inspect workplace is to inspect the **arrangement**, not the room. The gap here was not in the kitchen. It was in the placement.

## What Delhi should do

First, any placement of a person under 18 in a private home, through an agent or a relative, should be **registered with the district administration**, with the name of the agent, the employer's address and the wages agreed. A placement that is not on the register should be treated as trafficking until shown otherwise.

Second, wages for minors should be paid into a bank account the family can see, not carried back in cash or withheld.

Third, the district should make **scheduled welfare visits**, by the Child Welfare Committee or a trained social worker, within the first month and then every quarter. The visit should include a private conversation with the child.

Fourth, parents should be able to speak to the child on a video call, face uncovered, at a fixed interval. A placement that cannot allow this should not continue.

Fifth, the agents who move girls from villages into city homes should be licensed, and a licence should be lost on the first proven complaint.

Finally, the prosecution must be quick. A magistrate has already ordered the case registered under the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 and the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015. Delhi Police should say publicly when it has made an arrest and when the charge sheet will be filed. The employer's guilt is for a court to decide. The delay in finding out is for the state to explain.

The girl has told the CWC what she wants: to go back to school and become a teacher. A state that wants that outcome has to build the habit of looking in before a child has to be rescued.
