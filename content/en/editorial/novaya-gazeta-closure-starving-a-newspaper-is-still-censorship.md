On Monday the lights went out at 3 Potapovsky Lane in Moscow. **Novaya Gazeta**, founded in 1993 and the home of Russian journalism that asked the questions the state did not want asked, ended its work inside the country. The paper's own farewell statement was called "We Have Been Killed". It said the team would "turn off the lights in the home where we have lived for 30 years, shut down our servers, and leave".

The notable thing is how it happened. There was no decree naming the paper and no single day on which a minister signed it away. There was a patient sequence. By the paper's account, and that of the Committee to Protect Journalists, **its site was blocked and its print and media licences were revoked in 2022**. Its printing press passed to Moscow officials in 2023. Its reporter Oleg Roldugin was detained in April and then put under house arrest. In September the paper and its people were labelled "foreign agents". In October it was evicted, its donations platform was closed, and, the editors say, banks stopped dealing with it. Each step had its own paperwork and its own justification. Together they were a ban.

## Six names

The editors recall the cost of the years before: Igor Domnikov, Yuri Shchekochikhin, Anna Politkovskaya, Stas Markelov, Anastasia Baburova and Natalia Estemirova, who died for their work. Publisher Dmitry Muratov, who shared the 2021 Nobel Peace Prize, was attacked with paint that nearly cost him his sight. This is not a paper that was undone by bad business. It carried on through murder and threat, and was finally stopped by an administrative squeeze.

The UK and Finland, co-chairs of the Media Freedom Coalition, have called for **the immediate release of every jailed journalist** in Russia. The Committee to Protect Journalists counts 31 journalists detained or imprisoned there, the highest number since it began keeping records. Moscow has not answered.

## The fair objection

Governments do have lawful powers over media houses. They can regulate foreign funding, enforce tax rules and protect public order. Russia argues it is in an existential contest with the West and treats outside money as a threat. Not every country that registers foreign-funded organisations is silencing them.

But the test is not the label on the law, it is the result. When a regulator, a landlord, a bank and a printer all stop serving one newspaper in the space of a few years, and the newspaper is the one that reported on the state's security services, courts and war, then **the effect is a silenced press whatever the paperwork says**. That test applies in any country, including ours.

## What should follow

First, democracies should speak up for the people, not only the paper. The 31 jailed journalists have names, and they should be said at every bilateral meeting with Moscow, by governments that prize their ties as well as those that do not.

Second, India, which counts both a free press and a long friendship with Russia among its values, can hold the two together. Friends are allowed to say that jailing reporters is wrong, and a government confident of its own free press loses nothing by saying so.

Third, exile outlets deserve practical help. Journalists who now work from abroad need visas, banking access and protection from digital attacks. Readers inside Russia will find them only if the foreign platforms carrying them stay open.

Fourth, every country should check its own tools. A press can be starved by selective tax raids, loss of advertising from the state, or pressure on printers and payment channels. Laws that protect a newspaper's finances and a source's identity are cheap to write and expensive to lose.

Novaya Gazeta's editors said they would not stop being journalists. The best answer from the rest of us is to keep reading what they and their exiled colleagues publish, and to remember that a newspaper does not have to be banned to be killed.
