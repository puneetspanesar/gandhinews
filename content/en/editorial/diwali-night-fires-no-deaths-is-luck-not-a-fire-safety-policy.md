Nobody died. That is the sentence officials will take from Diwali night, and it is a good one. In Thane, **about 50 residents were brought out of a smoke-filled high-rise** by firefighters and the municipal disaster team, and Deputy Chief Minister Eknath Shinde, who went to the spot, said there were "zero casualties". In Delhi's Mohan Garden, seven people from three families were rescued from a burning four-storey home after firecrackers were set off. A rubber factory burned in Narela, and buildings caught fire in Ujjain, Dewas, Nagpur, Pune, Lucknow, Bhubaneswar and Surendranagar.

It is also a sentence that hides how thin the margin was. Three days earlier a fire in Karol Bagh's Gaffar Market damaged up to 45 shops in narrow lanes where engines struggled to get close. Fires of this kind are the rule of Indian city life, and the festival only concentrates them.

## What the numbers say

On Diwali night Delhi's fire service took 257 calls, of which **214 were fire-related**, and six people were hurt in fires. At AIIMS, the burns unit counted 42 Diwali-related cases by Monday morning, 11 of them major, nearly all from firecrackers. One eight-year-old was critical.

Over a full year the toll is far larger. The Lok Sabha was told in February that India recorded **6,891 deaths in 7,054 fire accidents in 2023**. The Minister of State for Home Affairs said fire safety is mainly a state responsibility, while the Centre has a ₹5,000 crore scheme, launched on July 4, 2023, to expand and modernise state fire services.

## The fair objection

Responders did well. Crews reached a tower in Thane at night and used drones to find trapped people. Fire services are stretched by a festival that produces hundreds of calls in a few hours, and many of the fires began with a cracker thrown from a balcony, not with a building defect. A building cannot be fireproofed against everyone's behaviour.

True, and that is exactly why the buildings matter more. When the ignition cannot be controlled, the escape has to be.

## What states should do

First, publish a **building-by-building fire clearance list**. Residents of a tower, a market or a hospital should be able to look up whether it holds a current fire certificate and when it was last inspected. A certificate nobody can see protects nobody.

Second, test the exits. Refuge floors, stairwells and fire doors in high-rises can end up locked, blocked or used as storage, and nobody checks until the night they are needed. Housing societies and market associations should be required to run a drill each year and file the result.

Third, treat markets as a separate problem. Lanes like Chain Wali Gali need clear access for engines, a ceiling on battery charging and stock in ground-floor shops, and electrical inspection on a schedule. Police suspect an overheated battery in the Gaffar Market fire; the cause is not confirmed, but the risk is familiar to every phone-accessory lane in the country.

Fourth, the cracker ban must be enforced in the days after the festival, not only on the night. Delhi-NCR's full prohibition returned on Monday, and the fires of Sunday are the reason to mean it.

Finally, fund the service. The Centre's modernisation money is only useful if states match it with hydraulic platforms, trained crews and water points in the places where fires actually happen.

A festival of lamps will always carry some risk. The aim is that the next quiet Diwali night owes its quiet to a building that was checked, not to a neighbour who woke in time.
