Three construction workers were killed on Thursday, November 5, at a factory site in the SIPCOT area near Hosur when **the scaffolding under a concrete structure gave way** during a pour. The Krishnagiri Collector, Dinesh Kumar, said 17 men were working on it. Three died under the rubble and at least four were taken to Hosur Government Hospital. Each family has been promised **₹8 lakh from the Tamil Nadu construction workers' welfare fund**.

The Collector has ordered the Deputy Superintendent of Police to take strict action against the engineer and others responsible, and an inquiry has begun. Early findings from the site, as reported, are that the structure had **no pillars or proper planning** to bear the weight of wet concrete. These are first findings, not a report. The inquiry must establish the facts and should publish them.

## The moment nobody checks

A concrete pour is the most dangerous hour in a building's life. Wet concrete is heavy, it cannot be taken back, and everything beneath it, the props, the shuttering, the platform, is temporary. When it fails, it fails with every worker standing on or below it. The permanent structure is usually drawn, approved and inspected. The temporary works that hold it up while it sets are too often decided on the site, by a contractor, from habit.

That is the gap Hosur points to. Action against the engineer after three deaths is right and necessary. It is also the one moment when the system is certain to act. What Tamil Nadu needs is a rule that makes someone answerable on **the Wednesday before the Thursday**.


## The fair objection

Builders will say a signed design for every pour is paperwork that slows small contractors, and that most pours go well. Engineers are in short supply outside the big cities, and an inspector who must visit every site before every pour could become a bottleneck or a source of petty bribes. Some will say the fault at Hosur lay with one engineer, and that new rules cannot fix individual negligence.

Part of that is fair. A rule for every garden wall would be absurd. But it does not argue against a rule for **large pours, arches, slabs above a set height or span and anything with workers beneath it**. Those are few, they are planned days ahead, and the cost of a design check is small beside the cost of three funerals and a shut site.

## What Tamil Nadu should do

First, require a **written temporary-works design signed by a named, qualified engineer** for every large pour in the state, kept on site and shown to the labour inspector on request.

Second, require a short inspection by a second person, not the contractor's own staff, before the pour starts: props in place, bracing complete, load path checked. The pour should not begin without a signed sheet.

Third, publish the Hosur inquiry. The district administration should state what failed, who signed what, and what action follows, so that every contractor in the state can read it.

Fourth, treat compensation as a floor. The welfare fund payment helps the families now. It does not replace a prosecution if negligence is proved.

The three men who died came from Hosur, Tirupattur and Tiruvannamalai. They went to work on an ordinary Thursday. A signed sheet and a second pair of eyes would have cost very little.
