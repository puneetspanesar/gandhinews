A voter in Rajasthan can be dead, shifted or untraceable on one list and a living resident of the same village on another. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is what the numbers say. The State Election Commission's rolls for the 2026 urban and panchayat polls carry about **5.45 crore voters**. The Election Commission's final roll after the Special Intensive Revision, published on February 21, has 5.15 crore. A study by the People's Union for Civil Liberties, which the rights activist Nikhil Dey repeated in Jaipur on Sunday, finds that **26.63 lakh names deleted in the SIR** are still on the local lists, while 15.52 lakh names kept by the SIR are missing from them. More than half of those missing are aged between 18 and 25.

Panchayat polling is running from October 25 to November 16. Rural Rajasthan is voting now, on one of these two lists.

## The commission's answer

The State Election Commission says it built its lists on the roll as it stood on January 1, 2025, as it traditionally does, and that its preparations began long before the SIR ended. A senior official has said no formal complaint was received and that corrections are not possible while an election is under way. The Chief Electoral Officer has pointed to a special campaign to add eligible citizens the SIR missed. None of that disputes the arithmetic.

The commission is on firm legal ground in one respect. The Supreme Court said on October 9, in an Andhra Pradesh case, that a revision of the rolls cannot be a reason to delay local body polls, and that existing rolls apply when new ones are not ready in time. We do not argue that Rajasthan's polls should stop. Delay would hurt the many voters who are on both lists.

## Why the gap still matters

The reasons recorded for the SIR deletions deserve attention. For 17.57 lakh of the 26.63 lakh, the stated reason was permanent migration, yet many still have Rajasthan addresses on the local lists. Women make up 58 per cent of the discrepancy group. In Bhilwara, 20.5 per cent of voters on the local rolls are not on the SIR roll. If the SIR was right, lakhs of people are voting who should not be. If the local rolls are right, lakhs of residents were struck from the national roll in error, and will be again unless someone looks.

Either way the state is running two sets of facts about who its voters are, and a citizen cannot tell which one a court, a candidate or a polling agent will treat as true.

## What should happen

First, the State Election Commission and the Election Commission should publish, constituency by constituency, the list of names that appear on one roll and not the other, in a searchable form, so each voter can check.

Second, the two bodies should do the booth-level social audit that Dey has demanded, with candidates' agents and civil society observers present, and publish the result.

Third, after the polls end on November 16, the State Election Commission should adopt one reconciled roll for all future local elections, with a written reason for every name removed.

Fourth, no voter whose name is on either roll should be turned away at a booth while the gap is being studied. The doubt should favour the voter.

The idea that an election roll is a settled public document is what lets citizens trust a result. When two official lists differ by the population of a small country, only openness will settle it.
