The Centre has said it will try something simple: keep some courtrooms open after the day's list is done. Arjun Ram Meghwal, the Union Minister of State for Law and Justice, said on Wednesday that **evening courts will start as a pilot in Delhi, Bihar and Andhra Pradesh**, presided over by retired judges, in collaboration with each High Court. If the pilot works, it will be taken to other states and Union Territories.

The case for trying is easy to make. Meghwal told Parliament on July 23 that **5.64 crore cases are pending** in the Supreme Court, the 25 High Courts and district courts. More than 96,000 are in the Supreme Court. In the High Courts, 80,660 cases have been pending for over 30 years. Judges are not going to be appointed fast enough to clear that by daytime sittings alone, and a litigant waiting a generation is not helped by a debate about method.

## The fair objection

Judges and lawyers will say, with reason, that this is a patch. Evening sittings tire the staff, strain court security and the Bar, and do nothing about vacant posts. The minister himself said in July that disposal is "within the exclusive domain of the judiciary", and the pilot depends on High Courts agreeing to it. A scheme announced in an interview is not yet a scheme.

That is exactly why the details matter. Nothing we could read says **what law lets retired judges preside, who pays them, how long they serve, or when the pilot begins**.

## What the pilot should publish

First, **the legal basis and the terms**. Before a single evening list is issued, each High Court and the Law Ministry should publish the order that creates the sittings, the powers of the retired judges and their pay.

Second, **the oldest cases first**. Evening sittings should be given the matters that have waited longest, starting with the cases over 30 years old, and the list should be public so that litigants can see where they stand. A pilot that picks easy cases will show good numbers and clear little.

Third, **monthly results**. The pilot's cases heard, decided and withdrawn should be reported separately from the regular courts every month, along with the cost per case. The National Judicial Data Grid already tracks pendency; it can carry one more category.

Fourth, no shortcut around fairness. Hearings should have the same recording, the same appeal rights and the same safety arrangements as day courts, and the small litigant should not be made to travel home late at night after a hearing.

Fifth, **a fixed review date**. The Centre has said it will assess the pilot before expanding it. It should name the date and the test: the number of old cases closed, not the number of sittings held.

## Judges still have to be appointed

Evening courts should not become a reason to delay filling judicial vacancies or building courtrooms. The Cabinet has already approved a plan for special courts, and our view on that is that judges and forensic labs come first. The same applies here. A retired judge in the evening is a useful addition. A permanent judge in the day is the system.

The idea deserves a fair trial, which means a trial that can be judged. If the Centre publishes the basis, the case list and the monthly figures, the country will know within a year whether the evening courts clear backlog or only add a line to the announcements.
