The Election Commission has done something sensible and overdue. In a letter dated November 9 to the chief secretaries and chief electoral officers of Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Goa and Manipur, it says the restrictions that bind serving government employees **also apply to retired officials** whom state governments have engaged or re-employed. Such people may not campaign or canvass, and may not use their name or position to favour a party or a person.

The gap was real. State governments across the country routinely bring back retired civil servants, police officers and technocrats as advisers, consultants and heads of commissions and corporations. Some of them sit close to ministers and know every officer in the district. A rule that bound the serving clerk but not the retired adviser who calls the same clerk to a meeting was a rule with a door left open.

## The mischief the Commission describes

A senior official cited in reports said the Commission had received complaints of retired officials engaged by states **convening meetings of officials involved in election work** during the poll period. No state or person was named. That is the heart of it. The danger is rarely a retired officer at a rally. It is a quiet call to a district officer, or a video meeting with the people who run the roll and the booths, from someone who answers to the party in power.

The letter therefore adds a second bar, aimed at the governments themselves: they may not summon election-related officers of a district where polls have been announced, or hold video conferences with them. Instructions on election work should come from the Commission and its officers, and from nobody else.

## Three things the letter should have done

First, it should be public. At the time of writing the text rests on reports of the letter, not on a document anyone can read. Voters, candidates and the officers it binds should not have to rely on summaries. The Commission should **put the full letter on its website** with the other instructions it has issued for these five states.

Second, it should come with a list. Whom does it cover? Each of the five governments should publish the names and posts of the retired officials it has engaged, and the bodies they head, so that a party or a citizen can see who is bound and notice if one of them breaks the rule. The letter extends the code to bodies funded wholly or partly by government and to their staff. Without a list, that is an invitation to argue about who counts.

Third, it should name a consequence. The Commission says any breach will be "viewed seriously" and asks chief secretaries for strict compliance. It lists **no penalty**. Serious viewing is not a sanction. The Commission should say what follows a breach: removal from the engagement, a direction to the state government, a reference to the relevant authority. It should also say where a complaint can be made and how quickly it will be answered.

## The timing problem

There is a further difficulty. The model code comes into force when the Commission announces a poll schedule. None of the five states has had its schedule announced, and the dates are not yet public. A retired official's influence on district machinery can be built in the months before, through postings, files and standing instructions that will outlast the code's first day.

The Commission cannot regulate the pre-announcement period by letter, and it should not pretend to. But it can **ask the five governments to disclose**, now, which retired officials they have engaged since the start of this year and in what roles. That would tell the public whether the gap the Commission has just closed was being used.

## A rule worth having

The Commission deserves credit for acting on complaints and for putting its instruction in writing. It would deserve more if the instruction could be read, checked and enforced by anyone it affects. A neutral umpire is trusted when its rules are public, not when they are explained afterwards.
