On Wednesday the Union Cabinet gave the Fast Track Special Courts scheme **five more years and 71 more courts**. The courts that try rape cases and sexual offences against children will now run until 31 March 2031, their number rising from 779 to 850, at a cost of ₹3,788.04 crore, most of it from the Nirbhaya Fund. Union Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said the aim was "to ensure faster justice in cases of rape and child sexual abuse".

Nobody should argue with that aim, or with the money. By the government's own count a dedicated court decides **about 9.5 cases a month**, against about 3.3 in a regular court. Since October 2019 these courts have disposed of more than 4.02 lakh cases. Many survivors who would otherwise have waited years have had a verdict.

But an extension is the easy part. The scheme's own numbers say it is losing ground.

## A backlog that keeps growing

Figures the Law Ministry placed before the Lok Sabha in July show that **pending cases rose to 2,45,579** at the end of 2025, from 2,04,122 a year earlier. In 2025, 1,43,936 new cases reached these courts and only 66,500 were decided, fewer than in either of the two years before. As of 30 April this year, 775 courts were working against a target of 790. Jharkhand left the scheme in July 2025 and now has none.

Seventy-one more courts will help. On their own they will not close the gap. At the rate the government cites, 850 courts would decide **roughly 97,000 cases a year**, well short of the number now arriving, and that is before anyone touches the 2.45 lakh already waiting.

## Why trials stall

A court is only as fast as the case file that reaches it. Rape and POCSO trials rest on medical and DNA evidence, and **forensic laboratories are the bottleneck** in state after state. In August 2024 the Punjab and Haryana High Court took up the problem on its own after learning that DNA reports were awaited in 73 cases in Faridabad alone, with the accused in 40 of them in custody for five years. Justice Harpreet Singh Brar spoke of the "indolent laxity" of investigating agencies and noted a shortage of laboratories and staff across Punjab, Haryana and Chandigarh.

Five years in jail without a verdict is justice for no one: not for a survivor who cannot move on, and not for an accused who may be innocent.

The courts also need people. The Centre pays for a judicial officer and support staff in each court, but **recruitment is left to states and High Courts**. A fast-track court without a presiding officer, a special public prosecutor or a stenographer is a room with a sign on the door.

## The fair objection

Speed has a cost. Rushed trials can mean a weak defence, careless handling of evidence and convictions that collapse on appeal. Child survivors in particular need patience and a court that does not make them tell their story again and again. A drive to clear numbers could do real harm.

That is a reason to measure the right things, not to move slowly. Delay is its own harm to witnesses: memories fade, families come under pressure and children grow up waiting.

## What the extension should come with

First, **forensic capacity should be funded alongside the courts**. Every district with a fast-track court should have assured access to a laboratory that returns DNA and medical reports within a fixed time, and any delay should be recorded in the case file.

Second, the Department of Justice dashboard that will track each court should be open to the public. Survivors, lawyers and researchers should be able to see how many cases a court holds, how old they are and whether its posts are filled.

Third, the Centre should release its share against working courts, not sanctioned ones, and help states that have stepped out, like Jharkhand, to come back in.

Fourth, the new courts should be judged by how quickly the oldest cases fall, not only by how many are disposed of.

The POCSO Act already says a trial should be completed, as far as possible, **within a year of the court taking cognisance**. Seven years into the scheme, that promise is still out of reach for most survivors. Five more years is a chance to keep it. Courts alone will not.
