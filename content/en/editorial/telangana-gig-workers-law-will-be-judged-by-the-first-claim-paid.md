On Saturday, Rahul Gandhi and Chief Minister Revanth Reddy rode pillion on delivery workers' scooters through Hyderabad and then stood before a crowd of riders, drivers and couriers to start the working of Telangana's gig workers law. The pictures were good. The promises were bigger: **₹5 lakh in accident insurance, ₹10 lakh in health insurance**, and a share of every transaction paid into the worker's own bank account.

The state deserves credit. It is among the first to write platform workers' welfare into a statute with its own board, a **unique worker ID that works across apps**, a grievance officer who must rule within 30 days, and fines for platforms that pay late. Millions of Indians now earn their living on a phone screen, with no employer, no leave and no cover when a wet road ends a shift. Someone had to start.

## Where the promise is still soft

The weak point is the money. The Act does not fix the fee that platforms must pay. It leaves the rate to a government notification, and when the Assembly debated the Bill in March the talk was of a levy of 1 to 2%. The **1% announced on Saturday** is therefore a figure put to workers at a rally, and it will become a duty only when the notification and rules say so, and say how it is collected, from which transactions, and how much of it reaches each worker.

The same is true of the insurance. The ₹5 lakh accident cover was first announced in December 2023. Nearly three years on, a rider wants to know who the insurer is, what the premium is, what a claim needs, and how long it takes. Cover cannot start until platforms register with the Board and hand over their workers' data, and until each worker is issued an ID.

## The fair objection

Platforms will say that a state-by-state fee makes them navigate a different rule in every market, and that costs end up with customers and riders. That worry is real. A levy of 1% on a low-margin delivery is not trivial, and companies could cut incentives or slow hiring to absorb it.

But that argues for clear rules, not for delay. The Centre's labour codes also speak of social security for gig and platform workers. If every state writes its own scheme, workers who cross a state line may lose their cover and platforms will lobby for the weakest version. The Centre and the states should settle a **common floor** for registration, benefits and portability, and let states build on it.

## What Telangana should do now

First, **notify the fee rate and the collection rules** at once, and say in plain language how the 1% is split between insurance, the pension promised, and the worker's account.

Second, name the insurer and publish the terms of the cover, including what counts as an accident at work and what documents a claim needs.

Third, publish **every quarter** how many platforms have registered, how many workers hold an ID, how much fee has been collected, and how many claims have been filed, paid and refused. Government estimates of the state's gig workforce run from about four lakh to about six lakh, and that gap alone shows why a counted register matters.

Fourth, extend the Gig Stops beyond the five now working, and put one where riders actually wait: near malls, hospitals and railway stations.

A law for workers is judged on the day a rider falls on a wet road and the claim is paid within days, with no agent in between. Until the first such claim is paid and the numbers are public, Saturday's launch is a promise, a good one, still waiting to be kept.
