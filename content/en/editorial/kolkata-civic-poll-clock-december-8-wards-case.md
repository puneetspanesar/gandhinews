Kolkata has been without an elected council since, by the petitioner's account, **June 8**, when the House was dissolved and an administrator took charge. On November 6 the Calcutta High Court stopped the State Election Commission from notifying the city's civic election until a challenge to a new ward map is decided. The order is defensible. What it leaves open is when.

## A clock the Constitution set

Article 243U(3) says an election to constitute a municipality must be completed "before the expiration of a period of six months from the date of its dissolution". Counted from June 8, that is **December 8, 30 days from today**. The Supreme Court has been plain about the administrator's place in this scheme. In the Pune Municipal Corporation case in 2022 it held that appointing an administrator "does not and cannot override the mandate of Article 243U".

The date rests on the petitioner's account, and no other dissolution date has been reported. Whichever way it is counted, the court has stopped the one step that would start the election, and nobody has said when it will be allowed again.

## The case deserves a proper hearing

The dispute is not trivial. On September 11 the State **raised the number of wards from 144 to 209**. Former CPI(M) councillor Dipu Das says the recommendation came from an administrator who was a Corporation employee and not an elected body, that the map is gerrymandered, and that it was drawn on electoral rolls and not on population, leaving many residents out. A flawed ward map would taint every result drawn from it.

The State has answers: that it can carry out delimitation without the Corporation's recommendation, that the administrator held the Corporation's powers, and that the challenge came late. The single judge, who had declined an interim stay on October 29, has yet to rule on any of this.


## The fair objection

It can be said that a court is right to hold up an election rather than let one run on a disputed map, and that a few weeks' delay is a small price for getting wards right. That is true, and the division bench was not wrong to act. But a stay is a means, not an end. When it carries no hearing date, it turns a six-month constitutional limit into an open-ended administrator's rule, and **residents of a city of this size lose their say** while lawyers wait for a listing.

## What should happen

First, the single judge should fix an early date for final hearing and say so in open court, with the State's affidavit due within days and not weeks.

Second, if the court finds the delimitation sound, the Election Commission should announce a schedule at once. If it finds it flawed, the order should set a deadline for fresh delimitation and a date by which the election must follow.

Third, if December 8 cannot be met, the State should say so on record and give its reasons, so that the delay is examined by a court and not simply allowed to happen. Kolkata should not learn of it from silence.

A municipality is the part of government closest to the pavement, the drain and the ration queue. The Constitution gave it a clock for a reason.
