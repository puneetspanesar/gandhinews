On Diwali the Prime Minister sat with CoBRA personnel at Karanpur in Bastar, a place that a few years ago was a byword for ambush. He said **no CRPF jawan died on duty** in the past year, the first such year in 47 years, and that this is the first Diwali when every district is free of Naxalism. Home Minister Amit Shah posted that it is the first Diwali in five decades free of the scourge. For the families of the 76 troopers killed at Tadmetla in 2010, and for the villagers who lived between the two sides, this is news worth welcoming without reservation.

It is also news that should be checked, because a victory that is announced in speeches can be lost in the gaps between them.

## Two figures that do not match

On March 30 Shah told the Lok Sabha that Naxal-affected districts had fallen from 126 in 2014 to **two**. Seven months later the Prime Minister spoke of every district. Both can be true if the last two have since been cleared, but the public has not been told so. The Chhattisgarh government said in March that 96 per cent of Bastar's area was free of Maoist influence and that only 30 to 40 cadres remained. Those are the kinds of numbers that need a date, a source and a place.

We therefore ask for something modest: the Home Ministry should publish a **district-wise list**, with the criteria used to call a district free, and update it every quarter. A claim of this size should be possible for any citizen to read and any court to test.

## The fair objection

Security officials will say that insisting on proof helps the remnants. A few armed cadres can still lay a mine, and no minister wants to announce an end that a single ambush contradicts. That is a reason for caution in wording, not for secrecy in method. Publishing the criteria does not reveal operations.

## What peace has to look like

The Prime Minister listed roads and towers: more than 15,000 km and 9,500, in his figures. Roads built with security cover are real gains. But the state's own promise in March was different and bigger. Deputy Chief Minister Vijay Sharma said about **400 interior security camps** would be phased out and turned into clinics, schools and centres for processing forest produce. That is the test the villagers will apply.

So the Chhattisgarh government should publish a timetable: which camps, which year, which department, with money allotted. Where a camp is converted, the building should open as a school or clinic with staff posted, not as a signboard.

Rehabilitation needs the same daylight. Shah set out the terms in Parliament: a surrender incentive of ₹50,000, doubled for groups, a stipend of ₹10,000 a month for 36 months, training and housing. In Bastar division alone, 2,756 people surrendered between January 2024 and March 2026. The state should say how many have received each benefit, how many have jobs, and how many have gone back to the forest. A policy that rewards surrender but loses track of the surrendered is not finished.

Finally, the language of victory should not become a licence. When the Prime Minister warned of "intellectual Naxals" in political parties and organisations, Congress's Jairam Ramesh called it an assault on institutional norms on a day meant to honour the forces. Whatever one thinks of that exchange, the forces' achievement is not served by turning a festival into a label for critics. Disagreeing with a policy is not an armed insurgency, and tribal communities who asked for forest rights or an inquiry should not be treated as its remnants.

Soldiers died so that a village could have a school. The measure of Bastar's victory is that the school opens, the road carries a bus, and the list of free districts is one anyone can read.
