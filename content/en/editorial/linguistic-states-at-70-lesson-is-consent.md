Karnataka celebrates its 70th Rajyotsava today, and Keralam turns 70. Madhya Pradesh marks its foundation day too, and Haryana turns 60. The coincidence is not an accident of the calendar. On **1 November 1956** the States Reorganisation Act came into force and India was redrawn into 14 states and six Union Territories, very largely along the lines of the languages people spoke.

It is worth remembering how unwelcome that idea once was in New Delhi.

## A map the leaders did not want

In 1948 the Dhar Commission advised against drawing states mainly on language, preferring administrative convenience and finance. A Congress committee of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya **set the linguistic principle aside** in 1949, citing the need for unity and security so soon after Partition. The fear was real: that states built around language would put loyalty to language above loyalty to the country.

What changed the government's mind was not a white paper. Potti Sreeramulu fasted for a Telugu-speaking state and **died on 15 December 1952**. Andhra State was created within a year, and a commission under Fazl Ali, with H N Kunzru and K M Panikkar, was set up to look at the whole map. Its report led to the 1956 Act. Bombay was split into Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960, and Punjab and Haryana in 1966, each after its own agitation.

## What seventy years showed

The doubters were wrong. Linguistic states did not pull India apart. They gave people governments that spoke their language, schools and courts that worked in it, and a pride in Kannada, Malayalam or Telugu that has sat comfortably beside being Indian. Many of the country's best-run states are the ones drawn on this map. The Union became stronger by **accepting a difference** it had first tried to put off.

That is the lesson worth carrying forward: India holds together by consent, not by insisting on uniformity. It applies whenever the Centre is tempted to settle questions of language from the top, whether over which languages children must learn or how a state names itself.

## Unfinished business

The 1956 map is not finished. Maharashtra has claimed **814 villages and towns** in Belagavi, Karwar and Nippani since the reorganisation. The Mahajan Commission found for Karnataka. Maharashtra took the dispute to the Supreme Court in 2004, and Karnataka still contests its right to bring the case at all. "Jurisdiction has to be decided first," Chief Minister Siddaramaiah said in January. A border row that still flares up, as it did with attacks on buses in February 2025, deserves an answer, starting with a ruling on that threshold question.

## The fair objection

There is an argument for leaving the map alone. Reopening borders invites new agitations, and later states such as Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Uttarakhand and Telangana were drawn on grounds other than language. Every concession, critics say, breeds the next demand.

But the history runs the other way. Demands that were refused did not disappear; they returned as fasts, riots and deaths, and were then conceded in haste. The better course is a **standing commission** that hears claims for new states and border changes on evidence, in public and on a timetable, so that no one has to die to be heard.

Seventy years ago India chose to trust its people with their own languages. It was a good bet. The Union should keep making it.
