The Supreme Court has suspended the life sentences of two men convicted of gang-rape and murder in the Bilkis Bano case. That is within its power, and the reasons reported so far are not frivolous. What is hard to defend is how it happened. The order is dated October 28. **It became public on November 16.** And Ms Bano says nobody told her. She learned from the news that two of the men who destroyed her family were about to walk out.

"I was not heard," she said in a statement this week. It was, she added, **the second time** an order in her case had been passed without calling or hearing her.

## What the court said, and what it did not

According to reports of the order, the bench of Justices MM Sundresh and PB Varale noted that Bipinchand Joshi and Pradip Modhiya had spent **more than 17 years in custody**, that one has a severe handicap, and that "there are issues to be decided in the appeal, both on fact and law". The convictions stand. The appeals against the 2017 High Court judgment are pending, and the trial court will set the bail terms.

Long custody and slow appeals are a real injustice of their own, and a court is right to weigh them. Nobody is arguing that a convict must stay in jail for ever because the crime was terrible. But the weighing is only as good as what is put in front of the judges. According to reports, the state opposed the plea. The person who carries the memory of 2002 was not there to say what release would mean for her and her family.

## The rule already exists

This is not a new idea. In April 2022, in *Jagjeet Singh v. Ashish Mishra*, the Supreme Court held that a victim "has a legally vested right to be heard at every step post the occurrence of an offence", and sent a bail application back to be decided after giving the victims a hearing. The judgment was written by Justice Surya Kant, now the Chief Justice of India.

That case was about bail before a trial. A plea to suspend a life sentence in a pending appeal is a different stage, and a court can say the principle does not travel that far. It should not say so quietly. The reasoning behind the 2022 ruling, that a victim's right of appeal means little without a right to be heard on release, applies at least as strongly to a survivor whose attackers have been convicted and whose case has been in court for 24 years.

## What should change

First, **notice to the victim** should be a condition for hearing any plea to suspend a sentence in a serious offence such as rape or murder. The registry can serve it, as it serves the state. If the victim cannot afford a lawyer, legal aid should be offered, as the 2022 judgment required.

Second, orders in such cases should be uploaded **on the day they are passed**. A gap of 19 days between an order and the public knowing of it is a gap in which a survivor can be left to find out from a phone alert.

Third, the court should say plainly, in the order itself, whether the victim was notified and heard. If she was not, it should say why.

## The objection

Some will say the victim is not a party to a criminal appeal, which is between the state and the accused, and that adding her would slow a system already drowning in delay. Neither point is trivial. But a hearing on one application, with notice and a short written response, adds days, not years. And the court has already accepted that a victim is more than a witness.

Ms Bano says she will challenge the order. That is her right, and it is a reminder of how much she has been made to carry. The Supreme Court restored faith in her case in 2024. It can protect that faith by making sure that **the next such order is not a surprise** to the person it affects most.
