The Supreme Court has said what victims of digital arrest scams have long wanted to hear: if a bank's due diligence failed, **the bank should pay**. The bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana wants the Reserve Bank of India's rules to require institutions to indemnify victims in an amount "not less than equivalent to the amount lost by the customer". Justice Bagchi put it plainly: "We will put up a strict liability on the financial institutions."

That is the right principle. The test now is whether the rule that follows lets a retired teacher or a pensioner get her money back without a fight.

## Why the principle matters

Digital arrest fraud works because the victim is isolated by a fake call from a "CBI" or "court" officer and told to move savings at once. In the case that started this hearing, an Ambala couple were made to transfer **more than ₹1.05 crore**. The bench has noted that victims have lost about **₹800 crore this year**. Those sums leave a bank account in large, unusual transfers, which is exactly what a bank's systems are built to notice.

The bench suggested what banks should do: pause an unusual transaction, alert the nearest police station and check it physically. These are not exotic. A customer who has never moved more than a few thousand rupees and suddenly sends lakhs should meet a human being before the money goes.

## What the rule must say

Strict liability on paper is not the same as money in the account. Three things should be written into the RBI's directions, which are proposed to be notified by **November 30** and to run from December 31.

First, a **repayment deadline**. A victim who files a complaint should be repaid within a fixed number of days, with the bank free to recover from the receiving accounts and from the fraudsters afterwards. Without a clock, "indemnify" will become a claims process that outlasts the victim's patience.

Second, a clear test for **due diligence**, written so the customer does not carry the burden of proving what happened inside the bank. If the bank saw an unusual pattern and let it through, that should be enough.

Third, **public numbers**. The court has asked WhatsApp to say what it will do and has told the Union to detail the 9,400 accounts blocked. Banks should be asked the same: how many suspicious transfers they paused, how many turned out to be fraud and how many victims they repaid. Without those counts, no one can tell whether the rule works.

## The other side

Banks will say that customers authorise these transfers themselves and that a blanket repayment duty invites careless claims. That is a fair worry and a reason for a precise test, not for leaving the victim out. A customer who is coerced by a fake officer for hours has not freely chosen to hand over a lifetime's savings, and the bank is the party that can see the pattern across thousands of accounts.

The platforms carry a share too. WhatsApp told the court it has acted against 9,400 accounts and is building software to flag impersonation. That is welcome. The court has asked platforms to send their proposals to the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre as well, so the police and the platforms work from the same list.

## What happens next

The case returns on **November 23, 2026**. The RBI must list all authorised financial institutions by then, and the court will look at the draft KYC directions before they are notified. That hearing is the moment to ask for the three things above. A principle announced from the bench will protect nobody until it is in the rulebook, with a deadline.
