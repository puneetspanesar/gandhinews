The most revealing thing to come out of Abu Dhabi so far is not a proposal. It is a disagreement about geography. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on Tuesday that Russian officers and intelligence representatives told negotiators they **already control about half** of the Donbas land that Ukraine still holds. His delegation rejected it and answered with video evidence and its own people on the ground. He also said Vladimir Putin had told the Americans Russia could take the whole east in **one to three months**, which he called untrue.

The talks, the first among Russia, Ukraine and the United States since February, moved to a second day on Wednesday. When this was written no outcome had been announced, and Moscow had not answered Zelenskyy's account in anything we have seen.

## You cannot split what you cannot see

Zelenskyy put it down to two sides living in different information spaces. That is more than a rhetorical point. Any territorial formula, including the idea discussed on day one that each side might give up a little land, assumes both sides agree what is being exchanged. If Moscow believes it holds half of the Ukrainian-held Donbas and Kyiv believes it holds all of it, the two are not bargaining over the same piece of earth.

Russia's long-standing demand is that Ukraine withdraw from the regions Moscow claimed in September 2022 but has not fully captured. Kyiv wants the line frozen where it is. Those positions are far apart even on a shared map. On two maps, a deal is not close, it is not even drafted.

## The fair objection

Wars are not ended by surveyors. Each side exaggerates its position at the table, and the claim of half the ground may be nothing more than a bargaining posture. Insisting on an agreed map before anything else could also become an excuse for delay, while people continue to die and a winter of strikes on cities and power lines approaches.

That is why the map should not hold up the work that can be done now. Talks on **prisoners and humanitarian steps**, which Zelenskyy described as a priority, do not depend on it, and a large exchange is reportedly under discussion.

## What the negotiators should do

First, the United States, as the party both sides are talking to, should offer a **shared, regularly updated picture of the front line** drawn from its own satellite and intelligence material, and both delegations should be asked to comment on it line by line.

Second, if a ceasefire or a freeze is agreed, an independent group, whether from the United Nations, a neutral state or both, should **monitor the line** and publish what it sees. A freeze that each side reads differently will collapse in days.

Third, the sequence should be settled openly: humanitarian steps first, then a ceasefire, then the harder questions of territory and security guarantees. Europe, which was not at the table on day one, has a stake in all three and should be part of the third.

Fourth, a meeting of leaders, which Kyiv says it will raise, should follow preparation and not replace it. A summit that begins with two accounts of the front invites a public quarrel.

## What India should say

India has repeatedly called for dialogue and diplomacy, and the talks give it something concrete to support. A settlement would matter in Indian homes through fuel and edible oil prices, and Black Sea trade routes feed both. New Delhi should say plainly that it **backs a prisoner exchange and a ceasefire**, and that borders cannot be decided until both parties accept the same picture of the front.

A war that began with a disputed claim on territory will not end on a disputed map. The first agreement worth reaching in Abu Dhabi is a single picture of where each army stands.
