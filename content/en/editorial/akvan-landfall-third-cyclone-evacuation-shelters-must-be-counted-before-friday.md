On Wednesday morning the India Meteorological Department (IMD) expected Cyclone Akvan to cross the coast between Nellore and Visakhapatnam. By the afternoon the stretch was **Narsapur to Puri, around noon on Friday, November 20**. That is a strip of coast hundreds of kilometres long, and it now takes in south Odisha as well as the Godavari delta.

The estimate of the storm's strength has moved too. One report of the IMD's outlook has a very severe cyclonic storm by Thursday evening, with winds up to 145 km/h. Another says the IMD lowered its landfall estimate to **winds of 90 to 100 km/h, gusting to 110**. We cannot tell readers which is right. Neither can the people being asked to leave their homes, and that is the point.

## What Odisha has done

Odisha has red warnings for 11 districts on November 19 and 20, from Malkangiri to Khordha. By Wednesday, **47,000 people had been moved**, about 25,000 of them in Ganjam, and 1,313 expectant mothers had been taken to health centres. The state says 448 cyclone shelters are open and more than 3,000 more are being readied, with ₹20.5 crore released in advance. Chief Minister Mohan Charan Majhi told officials he had given "strict instructions" to evacuate people living in foothill areas and said the aim was zero casualties.

This is early, and it is to the state's credit. But earlier reports of the same administration spoke of **about 2.04 lakh people** identified for possible evacuation. Against that number, 47,000 is under a quarter. Nobody outside the control room can say whether that is a plan on schedule or a plan behind it.

## Why a weaker wind forecast is not comfort

Cyclone Arnab came ashore on October 28. In Odisha it killed 11 people, and most of the deaths were **landslides in Ganjam and Kandhamal**, not wind. Six people are still missing. Akvan's red warnings cover the same hills, along with Gajapati, Rayagada and Koraput, and the heavy to very heavy rain is due on Thursday and Friday whatever the final wind speed turns out to be.

A downgrade in the wind estimate should therefore change nothing on the hill slopes. If anything, a storm now expected to push on into south Odisha puts more rain on soil already loosened by Arnab.

## The fair objection

Evacuation costs something. People leave harvest, cattle and homes, and a third storm in 21 days tests their patience. If the track shifts again, or the storm weakens, officials worry that people will not move the next time. The IMD itself says its track forecast is only moderately reliable.

That argues for being exact, not for waiting. Authorities should move people from places that are dangerous in any version of the forecast, the slopes and the low-lying villages, and be plain with the rest.

## What should happen before Friday noon

First, Odisha and Andhra Pradesh should each publish **a daily table, by district**: people listed for evacuation, people moved, shelters open, and shelters stocked with food, water and light. We found no comparable Akvan figure for Andhra, where seven coastal districts are under a red warning, though about 1.8 lakh people were moved before Arnab.

Second, the foothill and landslide-prone settlements in Ganjam, Gajapati and Kandhamal should be **cleared by Thursday night**, with the collectors saying in writing that they are.

Third, once the storm has crossed, each state should publish a count of the dead and missing with the cause of each death, within 48 hours. The lesson of Arnab is only learned if the cause is on the record.

Evacuation that is counted in public is evacuation that can be checked. On Friday, the number that matters is how many people were still on the slopes.
