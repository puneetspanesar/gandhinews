On Friday Brent crude ended the week near **$98 a barrel**, 16% below its October 30 peak of $118. On Sunday Secretary of State Marco Rubio called Iran's demands in the peace talks "outrageous and ridiculous" and said of the negotiations that "it hasn't been very productive". Tehran's foreign minister said the mediators had stopped travelling. Oil fell on the hope of a deal. The deal is not there yet.

India's policy should not depend on it.

## The relief is real, and fragile

For a country that buys about 88% of its oil abroad, a cheaper barrel matters. In April-September India paid **$90.6 billion for crude, 46% more than a year earlier**, for almost the same volume. The Indian basket averaged $116.09 in September, against $69.61 a year before. Roughly half of India's crude comes through the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway at the centre of the dispute.

So every dollar off the price helps the trade deficit and the rupee. But a fall driven by expectations can reverse in a day. A strike, a mined tanker or a collapse in the talks can add several dollars before the next fuel-price review. Analysts quoted this month said a Middle East escalation could add $5 to $10 a barrel.

## Frozen prices, hidden bills

The pump price does not move with any of this. Petrol and diesel were unchanged again on November 1. The cost is carried by the state-run retailers. According to ICRA, they lost about **₹12 a litre on diesel and ₹9 on petrol in October**, which comes to roughly ₹570 crore a day with cooking gas included. Indian Oil's finance director has said the loss on a cooking gas cylinder was about ₹229 in October and could reach ₹370 by December.

Oil companies posted strong profits for July-September, helped by refining margins. That is not an answer. A loss on selling fuel at the pump is partly offset by a gain from refining it, but the offset depends on margins that can narrow as quickly as they widened. In May the petroleum ministry said no proposal to support the companies was before the government. We have not seen a public statement since on who will pay if the losses continue.

## The fair case for holding prices

Holding prices is a defensible choice. A sudden rise in diesel feeds into freight, vegetables and farm costs, and families are already paying for dearer cooking gas. A government that lets the full shock reach consumers in a week would be criticised, and rightly. Analysts also note that retail losses are not the same as a company's overall loss.

The objection is not to protecting consumers. It is to doing it without saying what it costs, to whom, and for how long.

## What the Centre should do

First, **publish the under-recovery** every month, by fuel and by company, so Parliament and the public can see what price protection costs.

Second, say plainly who bears it: the companies, the budget, or the consumer in stages. A clear schedule is kinder than a sudden hike after an election.

Third, keep buying as if the strait could close again. Cheap oil is the time to add to the strategic reserve and to keep Russian, American and Gulf supply in the mix, even at a small premium.

Fourth, back the one thing that would end the problem. India speaks to both Washington and Tehran, and its ships and workers are among the first exposed. It should keep urging a deal and a safe passage through Hormuz.

A fall in oil on hope should be treated as a gift of time. The test is what the government does with it.
