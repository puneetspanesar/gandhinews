On Tuesday the administration stopped digging at Gidipabali. Seven bodies had been recovered from the village on the Ganjam-Kandhamal border, which the hill buried at about 3 am on October 28. **Six people remain missing**, and the families of those six were the ones who asked for the search to end.

It was a decision made in grief and it should be respected. About 100 personnel had worked on the site, with boulders the size of houses, no road for heavy machinery and, by the families' own account, **about 30 feet of rock and debris** over their homes. Further blasting risked fresh slides and the lives of the rescuers. Nobody should second-guess a family that has chosen to stop.

But stopping the search does not end the state's duty. It begins a different one.

## Missing is not a status the state can leave alone

A person who is not found is not, in the eyes of the law, dead. The ordinary rule presumes death only after a person has been missing for seven years. For the families at Gidipabali that would mean years without a death certificate, with land records, bank accounts, insurance and **compensation held up** by a question that everyone in the village has already answered.

Nine families told the Ganjam and Kandhamal collectors at the Kilapanga relief camp on November 3 that they wished to perform final rites for their missing relatives according to their customs. They should not then be told that the paperwork does not yet allow them to be mourned. Odisha should issue an order recognising the six as dead in this disaster, with certificates issued on that basis and the ex gratia paid in full, without a long inquiry into each case.

## Rebuilding is the next test

The families have also said they cannot live in temporary shelters for long and want **land to relocate to**. Disaster Management Minister Suresh Pujari has said the government will build houses and rehabilitate the residents. That is a pledge, not a plan. The village sits under a hillside that has just come down once and carries rain that may bring it down again.

The state should name the site, the date by which houses will be handed over and the person responsible, and say so in public. It should also decide what happens to the surviving families' farmland and cattle, which a relocation to a new village can quietly strand. Odisha has asked the Centre for ₹2,526 crore after Cyclone Arnab. Housing for Gidipabali is a small share of that sum, and a clear test of whether it is spent where the grief is.

## The dam log

The third duty is to answer a charge. Leader of the Opposition Naveen Patnaik wrote to Chief Minister Mohan Charan Majhi on Tuesday calling the Ganjam floods a "manmade crisis". He alleges that the India Meteorological Department gave its **earliest warning on October 23**, that dam levels should have been watched and water released before the heavy rain, and that the Water Resources Department instead released water after the rain had begun, without proper warning to people downstream.

These are allegations, made by a party with its own politics, and the government is entitled to dispute them. The state has so far pointed to the scale of the rainfall and the relief work under way. But this is a dispute that records can settle in an afternoon. When were reservoir levels read? When were the gates opened, and by how much? What was sent to the villages downstream, and when?

The state should publish the **gate-by-gate release log** for the dams in the Ganjam basin from October 23 to the end of the floods, with the warnings sent to district officials and the public. If the records clear the officials, the charge falls. If they do not, the families and farmers of Ganjam deserve to know.

## What a state owes

A hillside that comes down in a cyclone is a natural event. What follows it is a matter of administration: whether the dead are recorded, the living housed, and the decisions made upstream open to examination. At Gidipabali the search has ended. These three things have not been done.
