The promise sounds generous. At Belém last year, countries agreed to **at least triple adaptation finance by 2035**. Delegates in Antalya, where COP31 runs until November 20, will hear it repeated as proof that the rich world is listening. It deserves less applause than it will get, because the pledge does not say triple what, or who pays.

A UN report published on November 4 does the sum the pledge avoids. Even if the tripling is delivered in full, adaptation money reaching developing countries would be **$85 billion to $120 billion a year**. The estimated need is **$305 billion a year**. That leaves a gap of roughly $185 billion to $220 billion every year, on the pledge's own success.

## A target nobody can miss

The Belém text sets no baseline year. Without one, "triple" can be measured from whichever figure a donor finds convenient, including a figure that already counts loans, relabelled development aid or money first promised years ago. It names no payers, so no government is on the hook for a particular share. It sets no yearly steps, so the 2035 date lets everyone postpone the work to the last few years.

A pledge with those three gaps cannot fail, and that is its weakness. A donor that gives less can say the baseline was different. A donor that gives more can count it toward the target. The people waiting for sea walls, early warning systems and cooler schools are told that a number was agreed.

## What the loss and damage fund shows

The loss and damage fund is a small preview. On October 22 it voted **$20 million for Nepal**, its first direct response to a climate disaster. Nepal's estimated initial losses are $2.69 billion, so the grant covers less than 1% of them. In all, 119 countries have asked the fund for $2.8 billion, and its first funding round holds about $350 million.

Nobody doubts that the fund is better than nothing. But it shows how an agreed principle becomes a sum far below the claim on it when no one has written down who owes what.

## What India has asked for, and what it should add

India's Environment Minister, **Bhupender Yadav**, said at a briefing in New Delhi on November 3 that climate finance is India's first priority. India wants COP31 to launch a Climate Finance Work Programme and holds that finance is a developed-country obligation under Article 9.1 of the Paris framework. In Yadav's words, it "must be predictable, largely public and concessional".

That is the right argument, and the work programme is the right vehicle. It should be used to close the three gaps. India and the other developing countries should ask for:

- **A named baseline year** and a published figure for adaptation finance in that year, counted on one definition agreed by all parties.
- **A yearly reporting schedule**, so that progress towards tripling is visible each November and not discovered in 2035.
- **A separation of grants from loans** in every figure, since a loan that must be repaid is not the "largely public and concessional" money India has asked for.

The counter-argument deserves a hearing. Developed-country budgets are tight, the United States is staying out of the negotiations and some donors have cut climate aid. A rigid baseline may push some to promise less. That is a fair worry, but it is an argument for honest figures, not for vague ones. A smaller number that is real helps a coastal district more than a larger one that is not.

## What to watch

India should also open its own books. New Delhi can say it has spent X on adaptation from its own budget, on one definition, and ask others to do the same. A country that asks for transparency is stronger when it offers it.

The summit's leaders gather on November 11 and 12. If they leave Antalya having praised the tripling pledge and fixed nothing about its baseline, the next five years will go in arguing about the starting line.
