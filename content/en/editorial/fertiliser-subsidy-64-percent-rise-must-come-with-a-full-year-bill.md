The Union Cabinet on Wednesday approved **₹62,370 crore** to subsidise phosphatic and potassic (P&K) fertilisers for the rabi season that runs from October 1, 2026 to March 31, 2027. That is ₹24,418 crore, or 64%, more than the ₹37,952 crore set aside for last year's rabi. Farmers are not asked to pay more: the retail price of DAP stays at ₹1,350 for a 50-kg bag.

Protecting that price is right. Fertiliser is bought just before sowing, and a sudden jump in what a farmer pays at the shop cannot be reversed once wheat or mustard seed is in the ground. What reassures a farmer is the price on the bag, and the Cabinet has kept it where it was.

## Where the money is going

The rise comes from the world market. The Cabinet cited recent trends in international prices of DAP, muriate of potash and sulphur, and Union Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw linked the pressure to the West Asia crisis and the war in Ukraine. The per-kilogram subsidy shows how large the swing is. Nitrogen now carries **₹94.21 a kg**, against ₹47.32 for kharif 2026 and ₹43.02 for rabi 2025-26, so it has roughly doubled. Phosphorus is at ₹66.11 and sulphur at ₹10.42, which was ₹3.16 in kharif.

Supplies look comfortable for now. Vaishnaw put fertiliser stocks at about 23.5 million tonnes against an estimated rabi need of 35 million. A year ago stocks stood at 17.35 million tonnes at the same point.

## The question the Cabinet note leaves open

The Budget for 2026-27 provided **₹1,70,799 crore** for urea and nutrient-based subsidy together. Kharif's ₹41,534 crore and the new ₹62,370 crore add up to ₹1,03,904 crore for P&K fertilisers alone, which is about 61% of that combined figure. What remains, roughly ₹66,900 crore, has to cover a full year of urea.

The Cabinet release does not say what urea will cost this year, or whether the Finance Ministry expects to come back to Parliament for more money. A subsidy that grows by half from one season to the next is the kind of item that changes the Budget's arithmetic, and Parliament should hear about it from the government.


## The fair objection

A government that shields farmers from a price shock does not owe an apology for the cost. If the world price of DAP doubles and the shop price does not, someone has to pay the difference, and it is better that the exchequer pays it than that farmers skip a dose and sow a thinner crop. A fixed bag price also stops companies passing the shock on to farmers.

That is a good reason to pay the bill. It is not a reason to keep the bill out of sight.

## What the Centre should do

First, the Finance Ministry should publish the full-year estimate for urea and NBS, and say whether a supplementary demand for grants will be placed in the winter session of Parliament.

Second, the Department of Fertilizers should explain in one page why the nitrogen rate rose so sharply, with the import prices it relied on.

Third, the guidelines for the sulphur support, which pays producers 20% of procurement cost with floor prices of $800 a tonne for sulphur and $266.67 for sulphuric acid, should be published before the money moves.

Fourth, state agriculture departments should report every fortnight whether DAP is on the shelf at ₹1,350 in the districts that sow early. A protected price means little if the bag is not there.

A subsidy that shields farmers is a good use of public money. The public should be able to see how much of it there is.
