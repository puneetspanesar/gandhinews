A 16-year-old girl was freed on November 3 and 4 from a house in Abul Fazal Enclave-II in southeast Delhi. She told the Child Welfare Committee that she worked **from 6 am to 2 am**, was beaten over small mistakes and was burned with a hot iron on her back, hands and hips. She said she once went three days without food or water. Police have registered a case. At the last report, **no one had been arrested**.

These are allegations, and the investigation must establish what happened. What is already clear is how she was found. Not a school, a doctor, a neighbour or an inspector raised the alarm. An NGO, the Association for Voluntary Action, complained to the police, and the Kalkaji sub-divisional magistrate sent the Labour Department, police and NGO workers to the house. It is a good outcome. It also depended on luck.

## A year in a house no one could see

According to the NGO, another woman had placed the girl in the household, and she was paid only ₹18,000 for nearly a year. Her parents spoke to her on the phone two or three times, and her face was covered during those calls. Nobody was obliged to know she was there, who had brought her, or what she was being paid.

That is the gap. India's labour rules were written for factories, shops and mines, where an inspector can walk in. A home is private, and a live-in worker is the hardest worker to reach. The Labour Ministry told the Lok Sabha in July 2025 that domestic workers can register on the e-Shram portal by **self-declaration**. A girl locked in a kitchen cannot register herself. The same reply said States had been advised to regulate private placement agencies, and noted that India has not ratified the ILO's convention on domestic workers. Advice is not a register.

## The fair objection

There is a serious argument against doing more. Millions of families depend on domestic help, and most employers treat their workers decently. Inspectors entering homes raise real questions of privacy, and a heavy licensing regime may push the whole trade further underground, leaving workers with fewer protections, not more. Placement agencies and employers will complain of harassment, and some officials will exploit the power.

Those objections justify a light and careful system. They do not justify having none. Nobody is asked to open the cupboards of a household. They are asked to say, on a form, who works there and who sent them.

## What Delhi should do

First, register placement agencies and agents. Anyone who brings a worker from another state into a Delhi home should hold a licence and keep a written record of the worker's name, age, home address, the household, the pay and a parent's contact number.

Second, require households that take a live-in worker to register that worker, with proof of age, a written wage agreement and wages paid into the worker's own bank account. A child whose age cannot be shown should not be placed.

Third, give the Labour Department and Child Welfare Committee a duty of **follow-up visits** at fixed intervals, in the first month and then every few months, with the worker spoken to alone. A parent should also have a helpline number and a right to see their child at a fixed interval.

Fourth, make complaints easy. Resident welfare associations, doctors and neighbours should know whom to call, and a clinic that treats a child with burns should be told to report it.

None of this replaces the criminal case. If the allegations are proved, those responsible must face the full force of the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act and the Juvenile Justice Act. But punishment arrives after the damage. The girl told the committee she wants to go back to school and become a teacher. Systems exist so that a child does not need to be rescued before she can say so.
