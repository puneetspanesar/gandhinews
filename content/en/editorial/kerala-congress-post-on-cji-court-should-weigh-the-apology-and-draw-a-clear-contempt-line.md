A post on the Kerala Congress handle on October 12 told the Chief Justice of India, "Dear Surya Kant, If law and order is not your domain, resign now. We will find your replacement who can handle it." It was a discourteous and **ill-judged post**, and it appeared on the official account of a major political party. The party deleted it, and Kerala Pradesh Congress Committee president Sunny Joseph said it was put up by a media cell member without the leadership's approval.

On November 6 a Supreme Court bench of Justices Aravind Kumar and Vipul M Pancholi **issued a show-cause notice** to all the committee's office-bearers. It asked the Attorney General to assist, excused them from appearing in person, and said it had not yet expressed any view on the merits. The party's counsel told the court that an unconditional apology would be tendered. "We will see whether it has come from the pen or heart," Justice Kumar said.

## The court is right to defend itself

The bench's concern is fair. A party that tells a judge to quit while that judge's court is hearing a case about police conduct is not offering criticism of a judgment. It is trying to pressure a judge, and a court that shrugged this off would invite more of it. Judges do not have a platform to answer back.

The party cannot hide behind its junior staff either. An official handle speaks for the organisation. **The accountability sits with the leadership**, and an apology is a start, not a full answer.

## Where we would urge restraint

None of this means the harshest instrument should be the default. The bench has said that "any attempt to undermine the authority of the Court needs to be dealt with an iron hand". Yet the post was removed, the author was removed from his post and an apology was offered within days. In a democracy, **courts are expected to be criticised**, sometimes sharply, and the line between sharp criticism and contempt should not depend on how strongly a bench feels on the day.

The court has also now taken up several cases over posts and remarks about the CJI. A bench led by Justice Kumar has also called Shiv Sena (UBT) leader Sanjay Raut to answer a contempt plea, and notices have gone to journalist Nikhil Wagle. Each is different, but together they leave citizens guessing. They cannot tell whether a phrase like "resign now" is contempt, whether the author's rank matters, or whether a quick apology ends the matter.

## What we would ask

First, the court should **decide this case with a reasoned order** that states, in plain terms, the test for criminal contempt: what the words must do, or be likely to do, to the administration of justice. A line drawn once is worth more than a string of warnings.

Second, if the apology is found genuine, the court can accept it and record its disapproval. It should say clearly what weight it gave to the deletion, the removal of the author and the speed of the apology. That tells others what to do next time.

Third, political parties should publish **who controls their official handles** and what approval a post needs before it goes up. Every party in the country now runs a media operation that can say anything in seconds. A rule that senior leaders must be answerable for what goes out under the party's name costs nothing.

Fourth, parties should keep the argument on judgments, not on judges. They have every right to say a ruling was wrong, and to say why.

The case is still at the notice stage, and no next date has been reported. Whatever the outcome, the Supreme Court should not leave behind only a warning. It should leave a **rule that anyone can read and follow**.
