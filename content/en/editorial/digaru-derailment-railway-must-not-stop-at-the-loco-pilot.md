At 6:54 on Wednesday morning the locomotive and five coaches of the Kolkata-Sairang Express left the rails at Digaru, on the edge of Guwahati. Northeast Frontier Railway says **no passenger was injured**, and for that everyone should be grateful. Early reports of the injured turned out to be wrong, and the railway deserves credit for correcting them quickly.

The same statement also gave a cause. The train was being received at the station so that another could pass, and "the loco pilot could not control the speed". That is a preliminary account, offered within hours, before any inquiry had been announced in the statements available. It may well be right. It is also the most convenient explanation a railway can give, because it ends at one person.

## Why a driver is rarely the whole answer

A train arriving at a station to be held for another is not an unusual manoeuvre. It happens many times a day on a busy network. If it can end with a locomotive off the track, the useful questions go well beyond the cab. How fast was the train allowed to approach? What was the signal and the speed restriction? Was the driver rested, and how long had the driver been on duty? Did any system warn the driver, or brake the train if the driver did not? Was the track itself in order?

India has been here before. After the Kanchanjunga Express collision in June 2024, an inquiry found lapses at several levels and called the crash an accident waiting to happen. Blame placed on the person at the controls, however quickly, is where a safety investigation should begin and not where it ends.

## A bad week, not a single slip

Digaru is also not alone. Four coaches of the Karmabhoomi Express caught fire in Punjab on November 6; passengers were evacuated and no one was hurt. Reports mention a freight train derailing near Dhedela in Gujarat on Monday night and seven empty coaches rolling out of a yard in Tamil Nadu's Tirupattur district before derailing. None of these cost a life, and that is largely luck. A railway that carries millions every day cannot count on luck, least of all through the festive and wedding season when trains run full.

## The fair objection

The railway will say that a few incidents in a week, on a network of this size, do not make a crisis, and that a quick public explanation can cause injustice to a crew before the inquiry is done. Both points are fair. A preliminary finding should say what is known and what is not, and should never name a person as guilty.

But that argues for a better format, not for silence. At present the public learns the cause from a single early statement, and then very little. Inquiry reports, when they come, arrive months later and are rarely read.

## What the railway should do

First, every derailment, fire and collision, with or without injury, should have **a preliminary finding published within a fixed period**, say 30 days, giving the sequence, the speed, the signal aspect and the safety systems in place on that stretch.

Second, any inquiry should state at the outset whether it will examine the driver's duty hours and rest, the condition of the track, and the working of any train-protection system, so that the loco pilot is one of several questions, not the only one.

Third, the railway should publish a **monthly list of incidents with no casualties**, because that is where the patterns show. A derailment with no injuries is the cheapest lesson the system will ever be handed.

Fourth, the passengers of 13125, who were moved to another platform and then onto another train, should be told in writing what the inquiry found.

No one was hurt at Digaru. That is the moment to ask what would have happened if the train had been full and the speed a little higher, not the moment to move on.
