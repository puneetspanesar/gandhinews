On Saturday, the Union government closed Metro station gates across central Delhi, cut mobile internet around Jantar Mantar and detained thousands of people, including four former chief ministers, to stop a march by the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP). By Sunday the Supreme Court had to sit on a holiday to remind the authorities that people gathering peacefully cannot be held "save and except by procedure established by law".

That a court had to say this is the measure of how badly the weekend went.

## A right, not a favour

The Constitution gives every citizen the right "to assemble peaceably and without arms" and to move freely across the country. The state may limit both, but only reasonably and only for reasons such as public order. Six years ago, in Anuradha Bhasin, the Supreme Court said what that means in practice: restrictions must be necessary, the least restrictive option, limited in time and published, and the power to ban gatherings "cannot be used to suppress legitimate expression".

The Delhi Police had a real job to do. They say the CJP broke undertakings given at earlier protests. Stones were thrown near Akashvani Bhawan and Connaught Place, and more than 15 police personnel were reported injured. A force that has to keep a capital running is entitled to set conditions, fix routes and act against violence.

What it is not entitled to do is close a city in advance to keep people from arriving. The Metro shutdown hit every commuter in central Delhi, not just marchers. The internet cut fell on everyone within 4 km. Lawyers and journalists were detained doing their work. None of that is "least restrictive".

## What each side owes

This is not a contest one side can win by holding out. Each has something it can do this week.

```compare
{"title":"What each side should do now","columns":["What they want","What they should do"],"rows":[{"icon":"megaphone","label":"The CJP","cells":["The Chief Election Commissioner's resignation and a rollback of the SIR.","Commit in writing to peaceful, unarmed marches, accept a fixed venue and time, and expel anyone who throws a stone."]},{"icon":"police-car","label":"Delhi Police","cells":["Order in the capital and no repeat of violence.","Grant a time-bound permission at Jantar Mantar with conditions, and stop blanket preventive detention."]},{"icon":"scales","label":"Election Commission","cells":["To complete the SIR and remove ineligible names.","Publish constituency-wise deletion figures with reasons, and the dissent notes of its own members."]},{"icon":"bank","label":"Parliament","cells":["Its Winter Session agenda.","Debate the SIR on the floor of the House, where an opposition notice to remove the CEC is still pending in the Rajya Sabha."]}]}
```

The CJP has its own test to pass. A movement that began as satire, and whose protests preceded the Education Minister's resignation in July, has shown it can bring young people onto the streets. It now has to show it can keep them peaceful. A single stone hands the state its justification.

## The question underneath

The anger is not really about Jantar Mantar. It is about voter lists. Media counts put the names removed during the Special Intensive Revision at more than 130 million, and two of the Commission's own members are reported to have objected to SIR decisions at least 14 times in ten months. The Commission says the revision removes dead, duplicate and ineligible voters and that its decisions were taken properly.

If that is right, the Commission can prove it. Publishing deletions by constituency and by reason, and the notes its members wrote, would do more to calm the streets than any number of barricades.

The Supreme Court has done its part for now. When it hears the contempt plea on Tuesday, the Centre should arrive with more than an affidavit. It should arrive with a plan to let people protest, and an Election Commission ready to answer them.
