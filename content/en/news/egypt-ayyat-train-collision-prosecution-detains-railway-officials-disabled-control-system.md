Egypt's Public Prosecution has moved against railway staff after the November 8 collision at Al-Ayyat, south of Cairo, that **killed seven people and injured 125**, saying early findings point to "negligence and safety violations". Late on Monday, November 9, prosecutors ordered a station controller and a stationmaster at El-Raqqa detained, and the driver's assistant held until he can be questioned. They say the driver of the train that did the damage **had disabled its automatic control system**.

## What the prosecution says

The crash happened on the Cairo-Aswan line near El-Raqqa village in Al-Ayyat, Giza province, between the Kafr Ammar and El-Raqqa stations, about 52 km south of the capital. Passenger train No. 134, a Tahya Misr service from Giza to Fayoum, ran into the back of train No. 2010, which was travelling from Cairo to Aswan and, according to an eyewitness, was standing still. Several carriages derailed and some went down the embankment.

According to the prosecution's statement as reported in the Egyptian press, investigators inspected the site, reviewed surveillance footage and questioned witnesses, railway officials and injured passengers. Their preliminary conclusions are specific. The driver of train 134 **had switched off the automatic control system**. The controller and stationmaster at El-Raqqa admitted they had not been watching the control panel for signal tower T10. The controller is also accused of **tampering with the signals logbook** "in an attempt to conceal evidence" of his lapse.

No charges have been announced, and the length of the detentions has not been stated. The prosecution has also formed an engineering committee of professors from Cairo University's Faculty of Engineering to examine the railway system: signalling, maintenance, safety procedures and what it calls the human factor. The committee will look at earlier accidents to see whether the same causes keep returning. A separate inquiry is under way by the Administrative Prosecution, which handles misconduct by public employees.

## The toll

The Health Ministry's latest count is **7 dead and 125 injured**, with 132 casualties taken to hospital. Of those, 71 had been discharged and 43 were still being treated by Monday, and 11 had been transferred to hospitals in Beni Suef. Seventy-two ambulances were sent to the scene and patients were spread across the Al-Wasta, Al-Ayyat and Atfih hospitals, where doctors treated fractures and head injuries. A train driver was among those killed. The Red Crescent sent volunteers with relief supplies and counselling. Early reports had put the injured at 112 and then 120, and the figure moved as hospitals compiled their lists.

## Sisi orders accountability

President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi said on Sunday that he was following the accident "with great concern". He ordered an urgent investigation to "hold accountable anyone found to have been negligent". The Egyptian Railway Authority sent emergency crews and heavy cranes to clear the wreckage and restore services between Giza and Upper Egypt, which had been halted. Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry was among those expressing solidarity with Egypt.

Political pressure is building. **Calls for the transport minister to resign** grew on Sunday. Wahid Qorqar, who chairs the House of Representatives Transport and Communications Committee, asked Speaker Hisham Badawy to convene an urgent meeting, and the speaker agreed. Transport Minister Kamel al-Wazir and other government officials have been invited. The meeting is set for Tuesday, November 10.

## A corridor with a dark history

Al-Ayyat is the site of Egypt's worst rail disaster. In **February 2002 a fire swept through an overcrowded Cairo-to-Luxor train** there and killed more than 360 people, with some official figures above 370. A series of crashes in the same area in 2009 killed dozens more, and as recently as September 2026 two freight trains collided in the district, injuring a driver and his assistant.

The pattern of rear-end collisions is familiar. In March 2021 a crash in Sohag province killed at least 20 and injured 199, and investigators found the moving train's driver and assistant were not in the cabin at the time of impact; eight railway employees were initially ordered detained. In 2019 a collision at Ramses Station in Cairo killed at least 22.

Official statistics cited in the coverage show **severe rail accidents rose from 289 in 2020 to 752 in 2024**, even as the total number of reported crashes fell. The government has announced billions of Egyptian pounds in modernisation covering infrastructure, signalling and rolling stock, but the network remains large and old.

## Why it matters

For the thousands who ride the Cairo-Upper Egypt line every day, the prosecution's account points to failures that technology was meant to prevent: an automatic control system that was switched off, and a signal panel that went unwatched. If confirmed, the findings would put the focus on staff discipline and supervision as much as on ageing equipment, which is where the engineering committee's review of signalling and the human factor is aimed.

## What happens next

The House Transport Committee meets on **Tuesday, November 10** with the transport minister invited. The prosecution must still question the driver's assistant and decide whether to bring formal charges against the detained staff. The Cairo University engineering committee has no reported deadline. The Administrative Prosecution's separate inquiry continues, and the cause of the crash has not been formally determined.
