The Reserve Bank of India has found that **imported items drove about 18% of India's headline retail inflation in October 2026**, even though they make up only 8.5% of the new consumer price index basket. The finding is in an article in the RBI's monthly Bulletin for November, released in Mumbai on Wednesday, November 25, which also says the Indian economy "stood out" in a year of uneven global growth.

The article builds an import-intensity-weighted CPI on the new series, which uses 2024 as its base year. It puts a number on how much of today's price pressure arrives from abroad. October's headline reading was **6.09%**, up from 5.54% in September and above the RBI's 6% upper tolerance limit, as [we reported on November 12](/en/news/retail-inflation-october-2026-6-09-two-year-high).

## What the RBI says drives imported inflation

Food and fuel were the main channels of imported inflation during the Covid-19 pandemic and after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The bulletin says **precious metals have now become a notable source of imported core inflation**. Business Standard's account of the bulletin adds that higher precious metals prices have fed through since 2024.

That matters because gold and silver jewellery sit inside the core measure that the RBI watches for signs of a broad price trend. In October's data, silver jewellery was still rising 52.39% from a year earlier, down from 87.82% in September.

On crude oil, the bulletin says **the West Asia conflict caused a sharper shock than the Russia-Ukraine war**, but pass-through to retail fuel prices has been relatively limited. The RBI does not give price levels. Wholesale prices tell a different story: our report on October's wholesale index showed [fuel prices up 32.84% on the year](/en/news/wpi-inflation-october-2026), with headline wholesale inflation at 12.29%. Retail petrol and diesel have so far absorbed far less of that rise.

## An economy "stood out"

In the State of the Economy section, the bulletin says that "amidst uneven economic performance globally, the Indian economy stood out". It lists supply-side constraints, high energy prices and geopolitical tensions as weighing on the global outlook, and notes that several major central banks have tightened policy in recent months.

At home, high-frequency indicators showed resilience in October on strong credit demand. Bank credit grew 19.5% year on year at the end of October and deposits grew nearly 17%, according to the figures reported from the bulletin. Gross GST collections in October were the second-highest monthly level since the tax began in July 2017.

The trade deficit narrowed from a year earlier on strong exports, and external commercial borrowing and NRI deposit inflows stayed strong. Equity markets rebounded on solid earnings and foreign inflows, with overseas investors putting in nearly $14 billion in November so far after heavy outflows earlier in the year.

## Credit, NBFCs and US policy

A second article looks at non-bank lenders. As of the end of March 2026, **NBFCs kept double-digit year-on-year credit growth** and improved their asset quality. Industry and retail loans form the largest share of their books, with vehicle, gold and microfinance loans leading in retail. The growth in retail lending came mainly from loans against gold, which links the lenders directly to the metals price that the inflation article flags.

A third article studies how US monetary policy news reaches India. It finds that rising US yields, driven by news of monetary tightening, are associated with a weaker rupee, higher Indian 10-year bond yields and lower Sensex returns. News about US growth showed no statistically significant link to Indian financial variables, and India VIX showed no significant association with either kind of news. The Sensex effect is significant only in the conventional phase of US policy.

## How to read it

The RBI states that the authors' views in the Bulletin do not necessarily represent those of the Reserve Bank. The article is research, not a policy decision, and it announces no change to the repo rate or liquidity.

It nonetheless arrives at a sensitive moment. Economists quoted after the October inflation print expected the central bank to raise the repo rate again at its December review, and the finding that nearly a fifth of inflation is imported gives that debate a concrete number. If imported items stay costly, the part of inflation that domestic interest rates can reach is smaller than the headline suggests, since a rate hike does little to lower world gold or crude prices.

For households, the immediate effect is on what they already buy: jewellery, and fuel-linked costs such as transport and freight. The bulletin says pump prices have moved less than crude, so the larger effect so far shows up in jewellery and in wholesale costs that businesses may pass on later.

## What happens next

The next tests are the November retail inflation reading from the statistics ministry and the RBI's December policy review, where the monetary policy committee will weigh headline inflation against the imported share. The December Bulletin will carry the next State of the Economy assessment.
