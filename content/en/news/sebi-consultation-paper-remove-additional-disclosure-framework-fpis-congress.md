The Securities and Exchange Board of India has proposed **scrapping the Additional Disclosure Framework for foreign portfolio investors**, the rule that makes the biggest and most concentrated foreign funds disclose every person who owns or controls them. The regulator issued the consultation paper on November 20, 2026, and is taking public comments until December 11. The Congress has objected, saying the proposal reopens a gap that was closed after the Adani revelations of January 2023.

The paper is only a proposal. Nothing changes until SEBI's board considers the comments and decides.

## What SEBI proposes

The framework comes from Regulations 22(6) and 22(7) of the SEBI (FPI) Regulations, 2019, and an August 24, 2023 circular, later folded into the regulator's Master Circular of May 30, 2024. It applies to an FPI on either of two counts: **more than 50% of its Indian equity assets in a single corporate group**, or **more than ₹50,000 crore of equity assets**, alone or together with its investor group. The size limit was raised from ₹25,000 crore by a circular of April 9, 2025. An FPI caught by either test must disclose, with no ownership threshold, everyone who holds ownership, economic interest or control in it, down to natural persons. Broad-based pooled funds and government-related investors are exempt on conditions.

The paper makes **three proposals**: remove the framework for FPIs, remove its provisions for subscribers of offshore derivative instruments, and remove them for FPI segregated portfolios. It asks one question, whether commenters agree with removing the framework for FPIs.

## The regulator's case

SEBI argues the rule is stricter than anything abroad. Under the Prevention of Money Laundering (Maintenance of Records) Rules, 2005, India already identifies beneficial owners at a 10% or 15% threshold, depending on the type of client. The paper notes that the United States, the United Kingdom, Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong generally use 25%. The look-through rule has no ownership threshold at all.

The paper says FPIs withdrew **more than ₹5.61 lakh crore between October 2024 and October 2026**, and counts the disclosure rules as one factor, though not necessarily the only one. It lists practical problems: identifying large numbers of underlying investors, running separate funds for India, updating details often for hedge funds, and tracing owners in fund-of-funds and segregated portfolios. It also calls the concentration test an entry barrier, and points out that a single FPI's holding in a listed company is already capped at 10% of its shares.

The size test, it says, was meant to stop the FPI route being used to get around Press Note 3, the rule on investment from countries sharing a land border with India. Press Note 2 of 2026, issued on March 15, 2026, redefined "beneficial owner" under that rule to match the money-laundering thresholds, so the paper argues the extra disclosure for size breaches may no longer be needed. SEBI chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey had earlier said the regulator would soon bring a paper on simplifying FPI rules, including removing restrictions that other countries do not have.

## Congress objects

Congress general secretary (communications) **Jairam Ramesh said the proposals "move in the wrong direction"** and should be dropped. In a statement on X on November 22, he said the government wants a quick boost to foreign flows at the expense of market integrity. "India definitely needs foreign investors but not those with opaque and convoluted offshore ownership structures," he said.

Ramesh said the rule SEBI proposes to drop was "closed under pressure" after the January 2023 revelations and "is being reopened". He cited SEBI's September 2026 final order closing the Minimum Public Shareholding case against the Adani group, which he quoted as saying "the material on record does not establish" that Vinod Adani controlled the funds, while acknowledging circumstances that "may legitimately give rise to suspicion". He argued that the framework never applied to sovereign wealth, pension or other broad-based funds, so scrapping it would not reverse the outflows, and that SEBI's own paper concedes they have other causes. He also linked the proposal to the Prime Minister's remarks to industry at FICCI on November 19, which we reported in [our story on that address](/news/modi-ficci-agm-india-first-quality-gandhi-swadeshi).

No response from SEBI, the government or industry bodies had been reported at the time of writing. The claims about the Adani case are Ramesh's own.

## The timing

The paper arrives as money is flowing back. Foreign investors put a net **₹1.22 lakh crore into Indian shares in the first 22 days of November**, according to depository data, buying on every trading day except the Muhurat session. Other tallies, which stop on earlier days, put the figure between ₹1.12 lakh crore and ₹1.22 lakh crore. Either way it is the largest monthly inflow on record, above the previous high of November 2020. It follows net selling of ₹35,861 crore in September and ₹34,843 crore in October.

Geojit Investments' chief investment strategist V K Vijayakumar called the cumulative buying "an all-time record" and said it may reflect a shift away from AI-linked stocks. The reasons cited for the turn include lower crude prices, a firmer rupee and strong second-quarter earnings. Outflows for 2026 have narrowed to about ₹1.73 lakh crore from roughly ₹3 lakh crore a few weeks ago. The money leaving India for overseas ventures is the other side of the same account: our report on [October's overseas direct investment data](/news/odi-outward-fdi-october-4-65-billion-adani-loan-sun-pharma) shows Indian firms sent $4.65 billion abroad in the month.

## Why it matters

For investors in Indian shares, the framework decides how much is known about who stands behind the largest foreign holders of listed companies. For foreign funds, it decides whether a large India allocation triggers a look-through that many say they cannot meet. SEBI's own paper links the regime to part of ₹5.61 lakh crore of outflows over two years, which is why its removal is being weighed against the transparency it gives.

## What happens next

Comments can be sent through SEBI's public comments portal until **December 11, 2026**. SEBI's board will then decide whether to adopt the proposal, change it or drop it. A final rule would need a circular, and the part tied to Press Note 3 would need to be read with the government's own rules.
