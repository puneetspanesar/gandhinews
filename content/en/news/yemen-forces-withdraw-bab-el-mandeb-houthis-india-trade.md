Yemen's government-aligned Southern Armed Forces said on Sunday, November 15, that they were **pulling out of Dhubab Airport and the Bab el-Mandeb area** in western Taiz province, a retreat that leaves the Houthi movement dominant at the southern end of the Red Sea. The forces said the move was needed to secure Aden, the government's seat in the south. The strait, 29 km across, is the gateway for ships between Asia and Europe through the Suez Canal, and for India it is the main sea road to its biggest export markets.

According to reports from the region, the forces have redeployed to al-Suqyah, about 150 km west of Aden, while still trying to push the Houthis from nearby coastal areas. Saudi Arabia, which backs the government campaign, had not responded when the reports were filed, and no Houthi statement claiming the ground had been found by early Monday.

## What the forces said

In a statement on X, the Southern Armed Forces called the move **a necessary military adjustment**, one "to strengthen defensive lines and reorganize troop deployments". Another version of the statement said it was taken "based on the priority of securing the capital, Aden". They said their campaign would continue "until the insurgency is ended".

An unnamed military source quoted in the reports said the decision was driven by battlefield priorities, chiefly protecting Aden and neighbouring Lahj province, and was meant to stop Houthi fighters from outflanking government positions or cutting troop movements. Houthi fighters have meanwhile seized several positions in Lahj that overlook the roads towards Aden and the strait.

The pullback appears to end a **six-week counteroffensive** that began in October with Saudi-led coalition support. By one account, Dhubab has now changed hands three times: the Houthis took it in September, government-aligned forces retook it in October, and it has been left again.

## How the strait slipped away

The Houthis took the Red Sea coast and the strait in September, with reports at the time of the fall of the port of Mocha and of Perim island, which sits inside the channel. They had declared a blockade of Saudi shipping in the strait in July. Government forces launched what they called the "Dawn of Yemen" offensive in October; [as we reported on October 22](/en/news/yemen-taiz-fighting-260000-displaced-ocha), it was then claiming 835 strikes on Houthi positions while the UN counted nearly 260,000 people displaced since August.

The counteroffensive never dislodged the Houthis from the coast or retook Mocha. In the weeks since, the Houthis have stepped up missile and drone strikes on Saudi Arabia, including on airports and energy sites. They claimed strikes on Yanbu and the East-West pipeline on November 12, which Saudi authorities have not confirmed, as [we reported in October for an earlier round](/en/news/brent-109-houthi-yanbu-claims-hormuz-flows-russia-waiver).

## Taiz and the human cost

The city of Taiz remains under siege, according to the reports, with aid unable to get in and civilians unable to leave. It is sheltering roughly half of about 300,000 people displaced in the latest fighting. The UN human rights office had verified 251 civilian casualties by October 22, half of those killed being children.

## What it means for India

India has no troops in this war, but **its cargo moves through the strait**. An analysis published on September 21 put more than $200 billion of India's trade with Europe, North Africa and the Americas on the Bab el-Mandeb corridor, and said more than 20,000 Indian seafarers work on ships that pass through. An earlier analysis said Indian refiners supplied about 60% of Europe's diesel in August, most of it through the strait, and that pharmaceuticals, textiles, machinery and engineering goods also use the Suez route.

If shippers avoid the strait, the alternative is the Cape of Good Hope. The September analysis estimated that it adds **10 to 14 days to a voyage**, and a route comparison put Mumbai to Rotterdam at about 23,000 km round the Cape against about 15,700 km through Suez. Longer voyages mean higher freight rates, fuel and marine insurance, which exporters and, in the end, buyers of imported goods pay. One report counted 26 daily transits through the strait by October 18, still 29.4% below a year earlier, and put war-risk insurance more than 1,000% higher.

Not all analysts see a new threat at sea. Maritime expert Alexandru Cristian Hudisteanu said the Houthi gains **do not fundamentally change their capability** to attack ships, but make targeting vessels "simpler for them than before". In September a Houthi spokesperson said navigation through the strait would stay safe and uninterrupted, with operations limited to specific targets.

The human side has already reached Indians. [A yacht carrying 14 Indians](/en/news/fourteen-indians-yacht-yemen-safe-hodeidah) was attacked near Zuqar Island in October, and all 14 were later reported safe near Hodeidah, and the Ministry of External Affairs has repeatedly condemned the attacks on Saudi Arabia. In September, New Delhi said it stressed freedom of navigation and the safety of Indian nationals and was working with regional navies. No new Indian government statement on the withdrawal had been found by early Monday.

## A second chokepoint under pressure

The retreat comes while Iran is also disrupting the Strait of Hormuz, so two of the world's main oil routes are under pressure at once. Crude is flowing out of the Gulf at close to pre-war levels, according to reports, but ship attacks remain a daily risk and keep markets nervous. Saudi Arabia has leaned on its Red Sea terminal at Yanbu to bypass Hormuz, and an analysis cited in one report estimated that 70 to 75% of Yanbu's exports must pass through Bab el-Mandeb.

## What happens next

The next test is whether the Houthis press on towards Aden and Lahj, and whether Riyadh answers with fresh air strikes or a new offensive. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi is due in Oman on Tuesday, November 17, for talks with his Omani counterpart; Oman has mediated in the wider Gulf crisis. For Indian exporters, the signs to watch are war-risk insurance rates, daily strait transits and whether major shipping lines start routing round the Cape.
