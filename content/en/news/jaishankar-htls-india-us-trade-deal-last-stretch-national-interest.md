External Affairs Minister S Jaishankar said on Saturday that India and the United States are **at the last stretch of a trade deal**, with most issues settled and the remaining changes coming "more at the American end than our end". Speaking at a leadership summit in New Delhi, he said he hoped the two sides could "close it out soon".

It was the most direct update from the Indian side since US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in Mumbai on October 20 that the two countries were "about as close as you can be without having executed the agreement". Jaishankar did not give a date for a signing and named no specific item still open.

## What he said on the deal

Jaishankar said the talks had moved through the hardest part, the tariffs. "If you are serious, you go tariff line by tariff line, and that takes time," he said, describing a process in which **each tariff line has been worked through** rather than settled in a single bargain. He added that people become impatient at the final stage of any negotiation, and urged that the deal be seen in a broader frame because it touches jobs and livelihoods.

India, he said, wants an agreement that is beneficial and balanced, and that **gives it a comparative advantage over comparable economies** selling into the US, which remains India's largest market. He argued that earlier free trade agreements with competitive Asian economies had not delivered the expected gains, and that recent pacts have been assessed more carefully. He pointed to the agreements India has signed with EFTA, the United Kingdom, the European Union, the UAE, Oman and Mauritius, and said Indian exports are rising.

## How the talks got here

The negotiations began in early 2025. Since then, relations have been strained by US punitive tariffs, American objections to Indian purchases of Russian crude, and changes to US immigration and H1B visa rules. Our earlier report on the [talks reaching a plateau](/en/news/india-us-trade-talks-plateau-russia-oil-tariff-law) set out which US duties are in force and which are not, and our report on the [Mumbai meeting with Rubio](/en/news/rubio-jaishankar-mumbai-talks-close-to-trade-deal) recorded the US side saying only a small number of items remained.

Jaishankar also said Trump is pursuing many bilateral deals at once, so the challenge is finding common ground rather than any one disagreement. Asked about the US midterm elections, he declined to comment on another country's domestic politics. He said a Democratic majority in Congress from January would "of course" change something, but that trade negotiations fall within the administration's remit. He added that the shift in American policy is "not just a Trump issue", noting that similar re-industrialisation themes ran through the previous administration.

## National interest as the compass

On Ukraine and the Gulf, Jaishankar said **national interest will guide India's response**. "The compass for navigating it is our national interest," he said. He noted that the war in Ukraine has run into its fifth year and that disruption of Black Sea food exports affects global food prices. Of the Gulf, he said it was "inconceivable that what you see in the Gulf happened", describing countries firing missiles at one another's cities and leaderships. He did not name Iran.

He described an emerging world order that is more multipolar, with more centres of autonomous decision-making. In his words, alliances will be "less cohesive because of national interests and more transactional because they feed into each other". Competition will be sharper and national assertion stronger, and the world will be more complex and less predictable, though he did not call it anarchy.

## China, capacity and the road ahead

On China, Jaishankar said border infrastructure was "really neglected till about a decade ago" and has improved markedly since, and that India's capability along the border is better "in a very marked way". On the larger relationship, he said: "In asymmetry, you don't go toe-to-toe on every issue." He also argued that India missed the manufacturing waves of the 1960s to the late 1990s, and that the 1992 reforms only partly corrected those lost decades. India cannot catch up by following the same track, he said, and must leapfrog. He called Atmanirbhar Bharat and Make in India the **building blocks of foreign policy**.

## Why it matters

The US is India's largest export market, so the final shape of the deal decides the tariffs faced by Indian exporters of goods across sectors. Jaishankar's phrase that the remaining changes lie mostly on the American side also signals that New Delhi sees the next move as Washington's.

## What happens next

No signing date has been announced. Reports have pointed to a possible announcement around the G20 summit in Miami on December 14 and 15, where Prime Minister Narendra Modi and President Donald Trump may meet on the sidelines, as our report on [Modi's December travel](/en/news/modi-canada-visit-december-g20-florida-trip) noted. The Ministry of External Affairs has not confirmed the US visit.
