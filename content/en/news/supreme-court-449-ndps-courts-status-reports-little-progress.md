The Supreme Court on Monday asked the Centre, state governments and Union Territory administrations to **file fresh status reports** on setting up 449 exclusive special courts for trials under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, after the Centre's counsel told it that little progress had been made.

A bench of Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana heard the suo motu case on November 16, 2026. Additional Solicitor General Aishwarya Bhati, appearing for the Centre, said that **little progress had been made** under the court's recent orders. The bench said it would take the matter up again in a few weeks, preferably before the court's December winter vacation.

Justice Bagchi noted that the case is also a three-judge matter and that Chief Justice of India Surya Kant was not available for the hearing. The court had earlier voiced concern over a rise in narcotics offences and a growing pendency of NDPS cases.

## What the court ordered on September 7

The present hearing follows an order of September 7, 2026, in the suo motu case titled In Re: Creation of Special Exclusive Courts. A bench of the Chief Justice, Justice Bagchi and Justice Mohana then described the **"unfortunate alarming increase"** in NDPS offences and the pendency of such cases as the reason for urgency.

The Centre had told the court in an affidavit that **449 exclusive NDPS courts** are needed across the country. On September 7 the court was told that 176 of them had been set up. It directed the Centre, states and Union Territories to establish the rest, with **a target of six weeks**, and to provide the infrastructure and appoint the staff to run them. Six weeks from September 7 ended on October 19.

The two sets of courts in the case are handled differently. The NDPS courts are still short of the target. For trials under Section 11 of the National Investigation Agency (NIA) Act, the court noted on November 16 that its earlier directions, day-to-day trials and completion within a maximum of one year, have been **largely implemented**. Most states with more than 10 pending cases have exclusive courts, generally allotted no more than 10 to 12 cases each. On September 7, 22 exclusive NIA courts were working across the country, eight of them added since the previous hearing.

## State by state on the NIA courts

The bench went through the states one by one. The positions reported on Monday were these:

- **Karnataka:** three exclusive NIA courts are to be set up, at Bengaluru, Chikkamagaluru and Udupi. On September 7 the state had said they would be notified in about two weeks.
- **Tamil Nadu:** one of two proposed courts is working and the second is expected within weeks. The same expectation, two weeks, was recorded on September 7.
- **Kerala:** assured the court it will set up two exclusive courts. In September the court refused the state months of extra time and asked for them within one month.
- **Assam:** told to expedite two more courts. One is working. On September 7 it was given four weeks, with 26 cases pending.
- **West Bengal:** told to set up **three more courts, for a total of five**. Only two are working. Counsel for the Calcutta High Court said 50 cases are pending, not the 21 in the NIA's status report.
- **Jammu and Kashmir:** says it needs four courts for **47 pending cases**, but only one has been notified. The court had insisted in September on exclusive courts, not redesignated ones.

## Why the courts matter

An exclusive court hears only one kind of case, which is how the Supreme Court wants trials to run faster. In the NIA case it set the standard of day-to-day hearings and a year to finish, and it asked for roughly 10 to 15 cases per court. The same reasoning is behind its push for NDPS courts: a judge who has nothing else on the list can hear a drug case continuously instead of in pieces between other matters.

For people accused under the NDPS Act, a slow trial often means **long months in custody**, since the law's bail conditions are strict. The court's own judgment on November 5 in a Tripura case, which we reported, dealt with the 24-hour limit for producing an accused before a magistrate in an NDPS case. For victims and families, the other side of the same delay is a case that takes years to reach a verdict. Our editorial on fast-track courts argued that such courts need judges and forensic labs, not only a longer mandate.

The reports now ordered will show, state by state, how many of the 449 courts are working today, how many have judges and staff, and where the shortfall is. The September 7 count of 176 is the last figure the court has put on record. No updated number was reported from Monday's hearing.

## What happens next

The Centre, states and Union Territories must file their fresh status reports before the next listing. The bench said the suo motu case on exclusive special courts for NIA Act and NDPS Act trials will return **in a few weeks**, preferably before the winter vacation in December. No date was fixed in the reports of the hearing.
