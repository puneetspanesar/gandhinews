A US government study of more than 50,000 women has found that two of the best-known "forever chemicals", PFOA and PFOS, are linked to a sharply higher rate of **ER-negative breast cancer**, one of the more aggressive forms of the disease. The findings were published on November 12, 2026, in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, and they land in a country, India, that has no PFAS-specific limit for drinking water.

Women in the highest PFOS group had a **93% higher rate** of ER-negative breast cancer than women in the lowest group, close to double. For PFOA the gap was **67%**. The researchers found no clear link between either chemical and breast cancer overall, which is why the finding is confined to this one subtype.

## What the study did

The analysis used the Sister Study, run by the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, which enrolled **50,884 women aged 35 to 74** across the United States and Puerto Rico between 2003 and 2009. Each woman was free of breast cancer at the start but had a sister who had been diagnosed.

Researchers measured **17 PFAS compounds** in blood samples taken years before any diagnosis. They then compared 1,609 women who later developed breast cancer with 2,269 women in a comparison group, and checked the result against a meta-analysis of earlier research.

ER-negative tumours lack the oestrogen receptors that fuel many breast cancers. They tend to have poorer outcomes and few known risk factors, which is part of why a possible environmental cause matters.

"Certain legacy PFAS, including PFOA and PFOS, are associated with a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer," said Alexandra White, an NIH Stadtman Investigator and the lead author. First author Che-Jung Chang said exposure "is still potentially modifiable, meaning people can take steps to reduce their exposure".

The authors are careful about what the work cannot show. It finds an association, not proof of cause, and it did not test the biology directly. They suggest changes in gene activity or the immune system as possible routes. The percentages are relative differences between groups, **not an individual woman's absolute risk**. PFOA and PFOS have largely been phased out and replaced by other PFAS, but both are still measurable in most people. Whether the replacements carry the same risk is unknown, and White said more research is needed.

## Where India stands

PFAS are a family of man-made chemicals used in non-stick coatings, firefighting foams and water-resistant fabrics. They break down very slowly and travel through water. In November 2023 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classed PFOA as carcinogenic to humans and PFOS as possibly carcinogenic to humans.

India has **no PFAS limit in its drinking-water standard**, IS 10500:2012, which is under revision. The Bureau of Indian Standards has set up a working group on emerging contaminants and forever chemicals, but the compounds to be regulated and the testing methods are not yet settled. The Environment Ministry has confirmed that no specific rule bans PFAS manufacture. CSIR-IITR in Lucknow is part of a UNEP-funded plan to build baseline data and can detect 50 PFAS molecules.

What data exist are patchy. A study in the journal Environment International, led by Viktoria Müller of the University of Graz, sampled sediment at **14 sites along a 58 km stretch of the Ganga**, from Rishikesh through Haridwar to the Upper Ganges Canal near Roorkee. Standard testing found PFAS at every site, but the 40 compounds screened for explained **less than 1% of the total organic fluorine** present. Precursor compounds, which can later break down into regulated PFAS, made up as much as 28% in some samples.

"A large gap points to anthropogenic chemicals that routine monitoring overlooks," Müller said.

An IIT Madras study of 14 water samples collected in 2022 and 2023 found the highest PFAS levels in groundwater near garbage dumps, and levels 5% to 103% higher in treated water than in raw water. It tested only eight PFAS. A 2025 review covering Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal called contamination a "legitimate concern" but said its national extent is poorly defined. A modelling study by Rajneesh Gautam and colleagues projected that PFOA and PFOS in Indian surface waters could exceed US safety thresholds by more than tenfold by 2054.

## Reactions and what experts say

US Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the NIH study "links two forever chemicals to a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer".

In India, researchers have pointed to the monitoring gap rather than a single fix. Srimanti Duttagupta of the University of Georgia called the Ganga work baseline evidence for a country where PFAS data are sparse. Gautam argued for standard testing as the backbone of national monitoring, with wider methods at hotspots. A. Ramesh Kumar of CSIR-NEERI suggested limiting PFAS to essential uses, such as some medical applications. Satish Sinha of Toxics Link warned that industry may shift production to places with weaker oversight, and said that "even small steps, like creating a regulatory framework, move in the right direction."

## Why it matters and what next

For readers, the practical point is narrower than the headline. The NIH study does not say PFAS cause breast cancer, and it does not give an Indian woman a personal risk figure. What it adds is a second, large, blood-based signal from a US cohort for one hard-to-treat subtype, at a time when India has no set limit to test its tap water against.

The next steps are regulatory and scientific. The BIS working group has yet to fix which PFAS the revised drinking-water code will cover. On the research side, the NIH authors want newer replacement PFAS studied, and the Ganga work shows that testing for a short list of known compounds can miss almost all of the fluorine present.
