The Centre has approved **six new brownfield urea plants** at a cost of about ₹54,000 crore, the Department of Fertilizers said on Wednesday, November 25, in a bid to cut a growing dependence on imported urea. Each plant is meant to make **12.7 lakh tonnes a year** and to cost about ₹9,000 crore, and all six are cleared under the new National Investment Policy for Urea (NIPU) 2026.

The scale of the gap they are meant to close is large. India uses about **400 lakh tonnes** of urea a year, but domestic plants made 293.30 lakh tonnes in 2025-26, and the country's reassessed capacity for 2026-27 is 269.42 lakh tonnes a year. The shortfall of about 107 lakh tonnes is filled from abroad. Six plants of 12.7 lakh tonnes each add **76.2 lakh tonnes** on paper, enough to cover about seven-tenths of last year's gap, though less in net terms where a new plant replaces an old one.

## The six plants

The release lists two public sector plants, one cooperative and three private ones:

- **MFL Madras, Chennai (Tamil Nadu):** a new plant in place of the existing one, which is more than 60 years old and, the release says, costly to run, heavy on energy and a safety risk.
- **NFL Nangal (Punjab):** a modern plant to replace a public sector unit about 50 to 55 years old, whose capacity is 4.73 lakh tonnes a year.
- **KFL Shahjahanpur (Uttar Pradesh):** a cooperative project run by KRIBHCO, expanding a plant set up in 1995.
- **Matix Panagarh (West Bengal):** a private plant inside the 2017 complex. West Bengal has only one urea plant at present, and the release says the new one will help meet the state's need.
- **CFCL Gadepan, Kota (Rajasthan):** private expansion of a complex running since 1994.
- **IIPL Jagdishpur (Uttar Pradesh):** private expansion of a plant dating from 1988.

Building on existing sites is the point of the brownfield route. The release says the land, utilities, logistics and trained staff already in place should let projects finish faster and cheaper. It puts the saving from reusing that infrastructure at about **₹225 crore a year**, or about ₹1,800 crore over eight years.

## Why Nangal is on the list

The release uses Nangal to show what an old plant costs. It burns **6.498 gigacalories of energy for every tonne of urea**, against an industry weighted average of 5.95 and the new energy norm of 5. Since urea is made from natural gas, every gigacalorie above the norm is paid for in fuel, and the subsidy bill carries it.

## What NIPU 2026 changes

The policy is the successor to the New Investment Policy (NIP) 2012, announced on January 2, 2013, and amended on October 7, 2014. That policy produced six plants of the same 12.7 lakh tonne size: Ramagundam in Telangana, Gorakhpur, Sindri and Barauni through joint ventures of public sector companies, and Matix's Panagarh and Chambal's Gadepan III in the private sector. They added 76.2 lakh tonnes a year and lifted capacity from 207.54 lakh tonnes in 2014-15 to 269.42 lakh tonnes in 2026-27. The release does not say whether NIP 2012 is formally withdrawn.

The investor terms are new. NIPU 2026 sets a **return on equity of 12% to 16%**, with 12% as the floor and 16% as the ceiling. It also offers protection against currency swings: exchange rates will be normalised, and after four years the amounts will be converted into rupees at the rates then prevailing. Two other projects of 12.7 lakh tonnes are already under way, Talcher Fertilizers in Odisha, which uses coal gasification and is due by December 2027, and the Namrup project of Assam Valley Fertilizers and Chemical Company, announced on March 19, 2025.

## The import bill behind the decision

Imports have filled the gap in a costly way. The Fertiliser Association of India said on November 23 that India imported a record **about 10.35 million tonnes** of urea in 2025-26, and that imports in April to October 2026 were 7.20 million tonnes, 23% more than the 5.86 million tonnes a year earlier. Global prices have swung hard: about $947 a tonne in May, after the West Asia war, down to about $362 in October.

The Budget for 2026-27 sets aside about **₹1.17 lakh crore** for the urea subsidy, of which ₹91,000 crore is for home-made urea and ₹32,000 crore for imports. A year earlier the import subsidy was planned at ₹21,000 crore and ended at ₹52,000 crore. The association now expects the overall fertiliser subsidy to reach about ₹2.5 lakh crore against ₹1.71 lakh crore Budgeted, and its figures show that home-made urea can cost more than imports at present, up to ₹66,000 a tonne against about ₹35,000, because of gas prices. New plants will not lower that cost by themselves, which is why the energy norm matters.

The subsidy has been rising for months. The Cabinet raised the rabi phosphatic and potassic subsidy by 64% to ₹62,370 crore on November 11, and our editorial asked the Centre to publish the full-year urea bill. The Prime Minister said at a farm conference that about ₹16 lakh crore had gone into fertiliser support over 12 years. A UN warning that fertiliser supply through Hormuz was at risk showed how exposed imports are.

## What happens next

The release gives **no completion dates**, no bidding schedule and no financing breakdown for each site, and it says more projects will follow under the policy. The things to watch are the final terms signed with each company, the start of construction, and whether the pipeline in the release, including Talcher in December 2027, delivers on time. Imports will stay necessary until then: even with every plant built, the release itself expects only a marked fall in import dependence, not an end to it.
