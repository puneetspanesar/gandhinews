NASA's Perseverance rover has found **complex organic carbon in two ancient river rocks** in Jezero crater on Mars, according to a paper in the journal Science Advances that was summarised on November 12, 2026. The rocks come from the Bright Angel formation, the same site linked to NASA's September 2025 announcement of a possible biosignature. The authors are explicit that the carbon does not show Mars was ever inhabited.

## What the paper reports

The study, titled "Spatially distributed complex organic matter detected in an ancient river valley in Jezero crater, Mars", was co-led by Ashley E. Murphy, a postdoctoral researcher at the Planetary Science Institute, and Kyle Uckert, deputy principal investigator of the SHERLOC instrument at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Kevin P. Hand, Rohit Bhartia, Keyron Hickman-Lewis, Kenneth A. Farley and Benjamin P. Weiss are among the many co-authors.

The material is **macromolecular carbon**, large interconnected networks of carbon atoms of the kind also found in Earth rocks and in meteorites. SHERLOC, a laser instrument on the rover's arm that identifies minerals and organic matter without damaging the rock, picked it up in both rocks.

Two details make the result stand out. The carbon sits **only a few microns below the rock surface**, the shallowest detection of this kind reported on Mars. It also appears alongside carbonate and sulfate minerals, which suggests the organic material entered or formed in the rocks during at least two separate periods, probably when the mud was laid down and again when groundwater later altered it.

## Where the rocks come from

The rocks lie in mudstones of an ancient dried-up river channel, Neretva Vallis, which once fed a lake inside Jezero crater. Perseverance landed in the crater to study exactly this kind of setting. Murphy called the work "a resounding win for Mars science" and said "It is encouraging for Martian habitability."

The site is **more than 2,000 miles (about 3,200 km) from Gale crater**, where NASA's Curiosity rover earlier detected organic compounds. Finding organics at two places so far apart suggests they may have been widespread in the lakes and rivers of early Mars, the authors say.

## How we got here

Bright Angel is the same area where Perseverance photographed "leopard spot" markings on a rock nicknamed Cheyava Falls, in an image dated July 18, 2024. On Earth, such spots can form through reactions involving microbes, although non-biological explanations remain possible. NASA's September 2025 announcement described the site as holding a potential biosignature. The new paper adds a detailed look at the carbon in two of the rocks there.

## What it does not show

Macromolecular carbon can form through biological or non-biological processes, so **it does not show that Mars was inhabited**. The mechanism that produced it is still unknown. Murphy described the finding as "still one of the most exciting findings to date", while stressing the open question about its origin.

Chris Lintott, professor of astrophysics at the University of Oxford, put the caution in plain terms in a commentary: "if you see something life does, you can conclude life did it." On Mars, he wrote, the opposite is true, and every other explanation has to be ruled out first.

The preservation is itself a question. The authors say the carbon may have survived because it is chemically durable, because clays or iron-rich soil shielded it, or both.

## Why it matters and what comes next

The result tells mission planners where to look. Organic matter that survives close to the surface, in rocks that formed in flowing water, is a target for any future search for fossil microorganisms. Settling that question would need **laboratory instruments on Earth analysing returned samples**, not a rover's own tools.

Perseverance has already sealed more than two dozen samples for a possible return mission. A published report does not give the current status of the Mars Sample Return programme, and none of the sources used here sets a date for bringing the samples home. For now, the rover continues its survey of the crater and its river deposits.
