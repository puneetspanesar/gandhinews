Russia's best-known independent newspaper, **Novaya Gazeta, ceased operations inside the country on Monday, November 9, 2026**, ending a run of more than 30 years. The UK and Finland, co-chairs of the Media Freedom Coalition, issued a joint statement the same day calling on Moscow to end its repression of independent media and to free all jailed journalists.

## What the governments said

The statement, published by the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office on November 9, says the paper "has ceased its operations inside the country" after more than 30 years and calls its closure "a stark reminder of the sustained pressure facing independent journalism in Russia". It adds that the paper and its journalists reported "on corruption, repression and human rights abuses, often at great personal risk", and recalls the reporters whose work cost them their lives, naming Anna Politkovskaya.

The two countries say Russian authorities "continue to use repressive legislation to crack down on media freedom", and that independent journalists face "arrest, detention, harassment and political imprisonment". They call for **the immediate and unconditional release of all political prisoners, including all unlawfully imprisoned journalists**, and ask Russia to let reporters work "freely and safely" and to meet its international human rights obligations.

## The paper's own farewell

The date was set by the newspaper itself. In a statement titled "We Have Been Killed", published on October 30, the editorial team wrote: "On November 9th, we will turn off the lights in the home where we have lived for 30 years, shut down our servers, and leave." It blamed the Russian authorities and security services for forcing the closure.

The editors described **a coordinated campaign** that stripped them of every resource. They said they had been evicted from their premises, that the donations platform which let readers fund them had been shut down, and that banks had cut them off, which they attributed to rules set by the FSB, the state security service. The site had been blocked, the licence revoked and the printing press seized. The authorities had also labelled the paper and its staff "foreign agents".

The statement lists the cost of the years before. Six colleagues were murdered for their work: Igor Domnikov, Yuri Shchekochikhin, Anna Politkovskaya, Stas Markelov, Anastasia Baburova and Natalia Estemirova. Publisher Dmitry Muratov had acid paint thrown in his face and, the editors say, nearly lost his sight. Columnist Elena Milashina was abducted and beaten in Chechnya, and editor Oleg Roldugin was arrested and faces trial.

## How we got here

Novaya Gazeta was founded in 1993 in the spirit of glasnost, with part of the Nobel Peace Prize money of Mikhail Gorbachev. It became one of Russia's most influential papers through its investigations into corruption and its critical coverage of politics. Muratov, its co-founder and long-time editor-in-chief, shared the 2021 Nobel Peace Prize.

Pressure grew after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The editors say criminal charges for "discrediting the Russian army" left them unable to report on the most critical events of the time. The paper halted its print edition inside Russia in 2022 but kept publishing online from its Moscow office at 3 Potapovsky Lane until now. It was raided earlier this year and a senior editor has been prosecuted.

When the closure was announced on October 30, there was no immediate comment from the Russian authorities. The Kremlin has said in the past that some censorship is necessary while Russia is in what it calls an existential struggle with the West over Ukraine.

## What it means for readers

For readers inside Russia, the closure removes one of the last domestic outlets that investigated official corruption and the war without state approval. Its reporting on security services, courts and the regions now has no obvious replacement in the country. Journalists who have fled abroad continue to work from exile, and the UK and Finland said they stand in solidarity with them.

For the wider world, the case shows how financial pressure, eviction and the loss of donation channels can silence a newspaper without a single closing order, a method press-freedom groups will watch as other independent outlets come under similar pressure.

## What happens next

The Media Freedom Coalition has not announced further steps. The statement leaves open how the UK and Finland will press the demand for prisoner releases, and Moscow has not yet answered the call. Whether former staff and Muratov can restart some form of publication from abroad has not been announced.
