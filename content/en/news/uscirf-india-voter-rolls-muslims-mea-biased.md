The United States Commission on International Religious Freedom (USCIRF) said on Thursday, November 19, that **India is targeting Muslim citizens** by removing them from voter rolls, labelling them foreigners and now illegally deporting them. The Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) answered at its weekly briefing on Friday, November 20, calling the commission **a biased organisation with a blatantly political agenda** whose views need not be taken seriously.

## What the commission said

The statement came from Asif Mahmood, the chair of USCIRF. "India is targeting Muslim citizens by removing them from voter rolls, calling them 'foreigners,' and now illegally deporting them," he said. He tied this to remarks by Home Minister Amit Shah, adding that all of it was happening while Shah "has referred to India's Muslims as 'infiltrators' that must be expelled."

Commissioner Gene Mills went further. He called the expulsions of citizens on religious grounds "a shocking escalation of tactics that target religious minorities" and urged the US State Department to **designate India a country of particular concern**. That is the American label for governments that engage in or tolerate systematic, ongoing and egregious religious freedom violations.

The commission is an independent body set up by the US Congress. Its recommendations are advice to the executive branch and are not binding. Washington has not acted on its earlier calls to put India on the list.

## What India said

Asked about the statement at the briefing, MEA spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal gave a short answer. USCIRF, he said, is "a biased organisation with a blatantly political agenda," and its allegations should not be taken seriously. He did not take up the specific charges on voter rolls or deportations in his reply, and did not say whether the matter had been raised with American officials.

The line is a familiar one. On November 10, the ministry described USCIRF as a "biased and ill-informed organization" and said it stays silent on attacks on places of worship and hate crimes against the Indian community in the United States. Jaiswal repeated that comparison on November 20, pointing to attacks on religious places and racial abuse of Indians abroad as proof of what India sees as a selective approach.

## How the argument got here

The latest exchange sits on top of a longer one. USCIRF's 2026 annual report recommended that India be named a country of particular concern, citing "systematic, ongoing, and egregious" violations. On March 16, **the MEA rejected the report** as a "motivated and biased characterisation of India," said the commission "has persisted in presenting a distorted and selective picture of India," and told it to look instead at attacks on Hindu temples in the US and the growing intimidation of the Indian diaspora.

On November 14, India's ambassador in Washington told the panel to examine its own prejudices and stop lecturing others, after it posted about Sharjeel Imam, the activist who has spent more than six years in jail in the 2020 Delhi riots conspiracy case under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act. His trial has not begun. Reports quote Ambassador Vinay Mohan Kwatra as saying the commission had no standing on the constitutional rights of Indian citizens.

In its August 2026 country brief on India, which we read for this story, the commission had already set out the case it now repeats. The brief says the Special Intensive Revision **dropped over nine million names from the 2026 voter rolls** during the West Bengal campaign in March, that this disenfranchised millions of Muslims and other minorities, and that "infiltrators", the word used to justify it, is "a derogatory term used predominantly to refer to Muslims." It also says that after the May election result in West Bengal, the new state government began deporting Muslim migrants to Bangladesh, and that hundreds of Bengali-speaking Muslim Indian citizens were unlawfully expelled.

## The voter-roll revision behind it

The trigger is the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls. It began in Bihar in 2025, widened to 12 states and Union Territories in its second phase, and a **third phase covering 19 states and Union Territories** is under way. On November 2, Shah said three crore voters removed in the exercise were "Bangladeshi infiltrators." Opposition parties allege that about 13 crore voters have been deliberately taken off the rolls where the revision has run, and that genuine voters, many of them from minority communities, are among them.

In May, the Supreme Court upheld the legality of the revision but said the Election Commission cannot use it to decide citizenship. Since October 2, protests across the country have demanded the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. Some BJP-ruled states have used the exercise to deny ration cards to people dropped from the rolls, and in some cases police have refused to renew passports, according to reports. Our earlier reports on the [Delhi re-verification of deleted voters](/en/news/delhi-sir-reverification-47-lakh-deleted-voters) and the [INDIA bloc's questions to the Election Commission](/en/news/india-bloc-48-questions-election-commission-supreme-court-sir) cover those disputes in detail.

## Why it matters

For voters, the dispute is about who stays on the electoral roll and what follows when a name is dropped: access to ration, a passport, and the vote itself. The commission's statement does not change any of that. What it changes is the tone of the India-US conversation on rights, which both governments have tried to keep away from trade and defence talks.

For New Delhi, the practical risk is limited. USCIRF cannot impose sanctions; only the State Department can name a country of particular concern, and it has not named India in any year. But each statement gives members of the US Congress and diaspora groups a reference to cite, and the MEA has chosen to answer each one in public rather than ignore it.

## What happens next

No further step from either government has been announced. The State Department's decision on the country list, and any change to it, is made separately from the commission's yearly report. The Election Commission's third phase of the revision continues, and the Supreme Court's hearings on the exercise remain the venue where the charges about deleted names will be tested, not in Washington.
