A **fast stream of solar wind** is reaching Earth, and forecasters at the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) have kept a geomagnetic storm watch in place through Sunday, November 8. The watch is for **G1, a minor storm**, with a chance of G2, a moderate one, on Saturday and Sunday.

## A hole in the Sun's corona

The stream comes from a large, oddly shaped coronal hole, a dark region in the Sun's outer atmosphere where the magnetic field is open and charged particles stream out freely. The hole has been tracked since at least November 3. The UK Met Office forecast the wind at **550 to 650 km/s**, well above the usual 400 km/s.

NOAA put the peak of the first push at Kp 5, the G1 threshold, between 00:00 and 03:00 UTC on November 7, which is 5:30 am to 8:30 am IST. The agency's own wording, as quoted in forecasts, is that the field is "likely to reach G1 (Minor) geomagnetic storm levels, with a chance for G2 (Moderate) levels."

## Why the forecast is uncertain

How far the glow spreads depends on one thing forecasters cannot see in advance: whether the magnetic field carried by the wind points **southward**. Only when the stream passes the monitoring satellite at the L1 point, about 30 to 60 minutes before it hits Earth, do they know. A weak coronal mass ejection from November 1 may add a glancing blow.

An M3.5 flare from sunspot region AR4566 peaked at 18:33 UTC on November 5. The mass ejection it threw out was first judged not to be heading for Earth, pending a final analysis, so it is not part of the forecast.

## Who may see it

Under G1 conditions, auroras may show from places such as northern Michigan and Maine. If G2 levels arrive, they could be seen as far south as New York and Idaho, and in northern Scotland and Scandinavia. The new moon falls on November 9, so skies are dark. Aurora at this strength is not expected at Indian latitudes.

The Sun's activity is on the decline in Solar Cycle 25: the sunspot number was 45.7 in October, down from 60.3 in September.
