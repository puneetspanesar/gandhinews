India and nine other governments have signed a US-led pledge to protect the technologies and infrastructure that artificial intelligence depends on. The **San Francisco Declaration on the Secure Foundations of Enduring Prosperity** was signed on Sunday, November 15, at the Pax Silica Founders Retreat in San Francisco. The signatories are the United States, Australia, India, Israel, Japan, South Korea, Qatar, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom. South Korea's foreign ministry announced its own signature on Tuesday, November 17.

The meeting was convened by Jacob Helberg, the US Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs. It came two days before President Donald Trump is due to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, and on the day Quad leaders, Prime Minister Narendra Modi among them, are due to meet in Manila.

## What the declaration says

The State Department's text opens with a plain proposition: **"Economic security is fundamental to national security."** It then names two kinds of assets the signatories will protect. The first is "sensitive technologies": the goods, software, materials, knowledge, components and means of production used in what the text calls "super intelligence" supply chains. A footnote says some participants use that phrase to mean artificial intelligence. The second is critical infrastructure, both physical and digital: ports, logistics, communications, energy and ICT networks, along with the platforms that coordinate them.

The signatories describe protecting these as a shared responsibility. Reports of the text add that it calls for a **common framework for identifying such assets**, for diversifying supply chains, for tackling economic coercion and "distortive non-market practices", and for stronger national enforcement against undue foreign access or control. The State Department's own summary says "no nation can secure the foundations of the SI era alone" and that, less than a year after its launch, Pax Silica "is entering a new phase".

Five working groups are proposed: innovation-friendly policy, adoption of the technology across sectors, supply chain cooperation, "safe harbors" and supply chain security hubs, and the security and resilience of AI systems themselves.

## How Pax Silica got here

Washington launched Pax Silica in December 2025 to secure the supply chains for silicon and the other minerals used in semiconductors, and by extension AI. India has been part of the grouping for some months; a State Department release in February was headed "United States and India Sign Pax Silica Declaration". The San Francisco text is the first to set a common baseline for all the founding partners.

Some governments were less sure. Europe's governments had worried about broad definitions, their own regulatory autonomy and the "super intelligence" wording, according to reports on November 12. Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are **expected to sign in the week of November 23**, according to reports, though that is not confirmed.

## What each side says

Helberg framed the declaration as a matter of trust among partners. The coalition, the State Department said, "cements its shared principles into a working partnership with a common baseline", and "the security of each participant is strengthened by the vigilance of all". Helberg told a newspaper that partners have agreed to treat the security of those technologies and the infrastructure behind them as a shared responsibility.

South Korea was careful to limit what it had signed. Its foreign ministry, which sent Vice Foreign Minister Kim Jina to the retreat, said **"this declaration is a non-binding political statement"** and that "no specific country is mentioned in the text". Yoo Ji-young of the Korea National Diplomatic Academy read it differently, saying it was meant "to keep the U.S. side of the rope taut in negotiations with China". The comment was translated from Korean.

No Chinese government response to the declaration had been published at the time of writing, and neither had a statement from New Delhi.

## The China backdrop

The pact is part of a wider American effort to reduce dependence on China in areas it dominates, above all rare earths and other critical minerals. Washington has pressed Beijing to raise rare earth exports to the US and its allies. In October, China set out draft rules that would put [23 AI technologies on an export control list](/en/news/deepseek-minimax-clear-china-ai-export-registration), and in Washington a Senate committee has asked Nvidia's Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to [testify on chip exports](/en/news/senate-banking-lutnick-huang-testimony-nvidia-megaspeed-december-10).

Trump and Xi are expected to meet on **November 19 in Shenzhen**, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, to discuss extending a trade truce, including a suspension of export controls on some critical minerals. The [Rubio and Wang Yi call last week](/en/news/rubio-wang-yi-call-trump-china-visit-xi-florida-g20-trilateral) was the preparation for that meeting.

## Why it matters to India

For India the declaration matters less for what it obliges than for where it places the country. It puts India beside Japan, South Korea, Australia and the UK in a group that the US is organising around chip, AI and critical-mineral supply chains, at a time when Indian policy is trying to build semiconductor manufacturing and AI capacity at home. Membership gives Indian officials a seat in the five working groups, where rules on trusted suppliers, security hubs and "safe harbors" for supply chains will be drafted, and those rules would shape who Indian firms can buy equipment and materials from, and who can invest in them.

It also carries a diplomatic cost to weigh. The text is non-binding, but a pledge to strengthen enforcement against "undue foreign access or control" of sensitive technologies could, once translated into national rules, touch Indian companies' links with Chinese suppliers. Neither the declaration nor the reports on it say what India has committed to beyond the shared text.

## What happens next

The Quad leaders' meeting in Manila on Tuesday evening, November 17, is the next place the supply chain theme may surface, followed by the Trump-Xi meeting in Shenzhen on November 19. The working groups are to be set up under the declaration, and the European signatures are expected the week of November 23. Whether New Delhi issues its own statement on the declaration, and what it says about the working groups, is the thing to watch from the Indian side.
