The Winter Session of Parliament is likely to begin in the **first week of December**, according to reports quoting government sources on Sunday, with a Constitution amendment that links delimitation to a one-third quota for women the government's top legislative item. The dates have been discussed at the highest levels of government but have not been announced. The Lok Sabha Secretariat has issued no summons.

The reports point to a regular Session rather than the three-day special sitting on November 24 or 25 that officials had floated earlier in the week, a plan we covered in [our story of November 13](/en/news/priyanka-special-session-delimitation-not-informed-nov-24-25). The reports do not say whether that earlier plan has been dropped.

## What the Bill would do

According to the reports, the Bill would raise the strength of the Lok Sabha from 543 to a **maximum of 850 seats** through delimitation. That would allow 33 per cent reservation for women in Parliament and state legislatures to come into force before the 2029 general election. The reservation was passed in 2023, but under Article 334A it takes effect only after a delimitation based on a fresh census.

The government is said to be looking at a formula that would **raise every state's seats by 50 per cent** while keeping the share between states fixed on the 1971 Census. That answers the fear of southern states that a population-based redraw would cost them influence. The reports say no final decision has been taken.

## The arithmetic

A Constitution amendment needs a two-thirds majority of members present and voting. On a House of 543 with **three vacancies**, the reports put the figure at 361. The ruling NDA has 320 members, so it is about 41 short and needs support from outside its ranks.

A senior government functionary, speaking on Thursday, said the Bill would pass with a margin of **15 to 18 votes**. That is the same claim Home Minister Amit Shah is reported to have made to journalists on November 12, which Congress communications chief Jairam Ramesh dismissed as "bluff and bluster", as we reported in [our earlier story](/en/news/special-session-delimitation-bill-shah-361-votes-jairam-bluster). Nobody has named the parties that would supply the votes.

## How we got here

The proposal has failed once. The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was **defeated in the Lok Sabha on April 17**, by 298 votes to 230, the first time the Modi government lost a vote on a constitutional amendment. The two companion Bills did not proceed.

On October 19, Parliamentary Affairs Minister Kiren Rijiju wrote to Kharge that the government had consulted seven other parties after the Congress gave no dates, a [letter we reported](/en/news/rijiju-writes-kharge-delimitation-bill-consultations). On November 2, Shah repeated an offer of a special session, then on the electoral rolls, as [we carried at the time](/en/news/amit-shah-special-session-sir-dares-rahul-mock-parliament).

## Why the House can be recalled quickly

Both Houses were adjourned on August 13, the end of a Monsoon Session that, by PRS Legislative Research's count, saw the [Lok Sabha work for 15 per cent of its scheduled time](/en/news/monsoon-session-2026-ends-with-lowest-output). The Session was never prorogued. It has now stayed open for **94 days**, past the previous record of 87 days set in 2008.

That leaves room for an extended sitting of the existing Session at short notice. An extended sitting needs at least two days' notice, while a fresh Session needs at least 14 days. The Constitution sets no time limit for proroguing a House after it is adjourned sine die.

## The two sides

The political air is already charged. Opposition parties have stepped up their campaign against the **Special Intensive Revision** of electoral rolls and are demanding that Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar resign. The reports say they are expected to carry those protests into Parliament.

A member of the ruling alliance told the reporters that a debate and vote on a Constitution amendment would not be possible unless the House works in an orderly manner. That is a pointed remark after a Monsoon Session in which nine of the 11 Bills passed in the Lok Sabha were taken with no member other than the minister speaking.

The Congress has not opposed women's reservation. Its position, set out in letters from Kharge, is that the quota should apply within the existing 543 seats and that no Bill should be pushed through again without an **all-party meeting**. The government's case is that delimitation is a constitutional necessity and the only way to give women their one-third before 2029.

## Why it matters

For women, the Bill decides when the 2023 promise starts to count: without a delimitation, the reservation waits for the next census, whose reference date is March 1, 2027, and a delimitation on its basis is unlikely to be finished before the 2029 election. For states, the seat formula decides how much weight each carries in the House for the next generation. For the House itself, a Session that opens in the shadow of the electoral roll dispute will test whether any Bill can be debated at all.

## What happens next

Nothing is fixed until the Lok Sabha Secretariat issues a summons, and the dates in the reports are described only as the likely window. If the government chooses to extend the old Session, two days' notice is enough. If it calls a new one, 14 days' notice means a summons by about **November 20 or 21** for a sitting in the first week of December. The text of any seat formula would be the first thing to watch, since the April Bill did not contain a 50 per cent increase.
