The Reserve Bank of India has asked the Supreme Court to review its own defeat over YES Bank's **₹8,415 crore AT-1 bond write-down**, and wants the October 15 judgment put on hold while the Court decides. The regulator filed its review petition on **Friday, November 13**, according to the Court's website, and YES Bank has filed one of its own. The bondholders' trustee, meanwhile, has gone to the Court for contempt.

The judgment under challenge, by Justices Dipankar Datta and Augustine George Masih, upheld the Bombay High Court's January 2023 ruling that the March 2020 write-down was void. Filing a review petition does not suspend that order. As of Thursday morning, November 19, no hearing date for either petition had been reported.

## What the RBI is asking for

According to reports on the petition, the RBI says the bench **did not adequately weigh its Basel III Master Circular** on AT-1 bonds and their loss-absorption mechanism, and that the judgment blurs the line between a pre-specified trigger and the point-of-non-viability trigger, the two ways such bonds can be written down. The regulator has asked for an open-court hearing and a stay of the October 15 judgment while the plea is considered.

Other accounts say the RBI also contests the Court's reading that the Centre's deletion of the write-down clause from the final 2020 reconstruction scheme was a deliberate decision not to write the bonds down, and objects to being described as a "bystander". A market note on Thursday reports the regulator's concern that **restoring the bonds could hurt depositors** and future bank-recovery efforts; that is a single report and the petition's text is not public.

The ordinary path for such a plea is narrow. Review petitions are usually decided in chambers by the same bench that gave the judgment, and the Court reopens a matter only in exceptional cases.

## What YES Bank says

The bank sought reconsideration under Article 137 of the Constitution and disclosed the filing to the stock exchanges on November 13. It did not set out its grounds in that disclosure. Its figures show why it is fighting. Restoring the principal could cut its common equity Tier-1 (CET1) ratio by about **250 basis points**, on its June 2026 capital position.

On a pro forma basis, the bank's September-quarter profit of ₹1,080 crore would become **a loss of ₹5,217 crore**, its net worth would fall from ₹53,268 crore to ₹46,970 crore, and its net borrowings would rise from ₹77,734 crore to ₹86,149 crore. Those are the bank's own estimates, not revised results, and its auditors had already qualified the quarter's accounts because they leave the ruling out. Our report on the results sets out the numbers.

## The bondholders' side

Axis Trustee Services, which represents the bondholders and began the litigation in May 2020, wrote to YES Bank on **October 28** asking it to restore the bonds within three days. The bank replied on October 30 that it was examining the judgment and its legal options. The trustee e-filed a **contempt petition on November 6** against the bank, certain officials and directors, saying the bank had not given a concrete roadmap for compliance. It wants the bonds, their associated rights and the relevant depository and exchange records restored.

The coupon is a second sore point. A payment on the 9% bonds fell due on October 18; YES Bank said it was assessing the matter and that the bond terms leave it discretion over coupons.

## The bonds and the ruling

The ₹8,415 crore covers two issues: ₹3,000 crore raised in December 2016 at 9.5% and ₹5,415 crore in October 2017 at 9%. The administrator wrote them down on March 14, 2020, a day after the reconstruction scheme came into force. Our report on the full judgment records the bench's finding that he then had **no authority to do so**, and its remark that the RBI had stood as a "mere bystander" while the bank was run in a clandestine manner. The bench did not rule out AT-1 write-downs in general; it said they must follow the prescribed procedure. Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation now holds 24% of the bank.

## Why it matters

For bondholders, many of them individuals who were sold the bonds, the review decides whether a High Court win of January 2023 turns into payment or into another round. For YES Bank's shareholders, the stakes are capital: the bank has said the ruling would erase its quarterly profit on paper and thin its core capital. For the wider market, the RBI's argument that the Court misread its AT-1 rules matters beyond one lender, because the instruments are written into every bank's capital structure. Our explainer on AT-1 bonds sets out how they work.

## What happens next

The Supreme Court must first decide whether to entertain either review petition, and whether to hear the RBI's plea for a stay in open court. The contempt petition is pending separately. Until the Court says otherwise, the October 15 judgment stands, and the bank must still say how it will restore the bonds and when it will pay the disputed coupon.
