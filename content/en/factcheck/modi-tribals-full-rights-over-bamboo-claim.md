## What we found

Narendra Modi's account is **misleading**. The Indian Forest Act of 1927 did count bamboos as a tree, and his government's 2017 amendment did take them out of that definition. But the change covers **bamboo grown in non-forest areas**, and the right of forest-dwelling communities to own and sell bamboo was written into law in 2006, under the previous government. "Full rights" is more than either law says.

## The claim

Addressing the Janjatiya Gaurav Diwas programme at Gadchiroli by video on November 15, 2026, Modi said the British had classified bamboo as a tree, so that cutting it could land a tribal in jail, that the law should have been scrapped after Independence, and that his government ended it and made sure tribals have full rights over bamboo.

## The evidence

### 1. The 1927 definition and the 2017 change

PRS Legislative Research, which tracks bills, records that the 1927 Act defined "tree" to include palms, bamboos, stumps, brush-wood and canes. Because bamboo was a tree, felling it or carrying it between places needed permission. The Indian Forest (Amendment) Ordinance was promulgated on **November 23, 2017**. A Bill to replace it was introduced in the Lok Sabha on December 18, 2017, by the then Environment Minister Harsh Vardhan, passed there on December 20 and passed by the Rajya Sabha on December 27. PRS gives January 5, 2018 as the date the Act replaced the ordinance.

### 2. What the amendment covers

The change removes the word "bamboos" from the definition of tree. As a result, bamboo grown in non-forest areas no longer needs a permit for felling or transport for economic use. The PRS pages do not say how bamboo inside forests is treated, and we could not open the ministry's release or the parliamentary debate to find out. So the amendment is real and is Modi's government's, but it is a **permit exemption for non-forest bamboo**, not a transfer of rights to tribals as such.

### 3. What the Forest Rights Act already said

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 defines minor forest produce in section 2(i) as non-timber forest produce of plant origin "including bamboo, brush wood, stumps, cane". Section 3(1)(c) then gives forest-dwelling communities the "right of ownership, access to collect, use, and dispose of minor forest produce" that has been traditionally collected. The Act was passed in December 2006 and carries the number 2 of 2007. The tribal right to bamboo as forest produce therefore **predates the 2017 amendment by more than a decade**.

### 4. Why "full rights" overreaches

Rights under the 2006 Act depend on claims being recognised by village and district bodies, and the 2017 change applies to non-forest land. Neither is a blanket right. We did not test how many families hold recognised rights, so this check does not say how far the promise has reached on the ground.

## Why this claim spread

The speech came on the 151st birth anniversary of Birsa Munda, when the Prime Minister opened tribal projects worth about Rs 3,000 crore. The bamboo remark is a simple before-and-after story that fits such a day. The detail that the Forest Rights Act already named bamboo is less often mentioned.

## What would change this rating

If the 2018 Act or later rules extended a general grant of rights over bamboo to tribal communities inside forests, the rating could move to Mostly true. If the Forest Rights Act text had not named bamboo, it would move toward True. The present rating rests on the text of both laws as opened, with the forest-area treatment after 2017 left untested.
