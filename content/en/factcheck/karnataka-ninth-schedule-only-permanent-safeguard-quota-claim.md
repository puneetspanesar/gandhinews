## What we found

The claim is misleading. In its release of November 11, 2026, announcing the appeal in the Supreme Court, Karnataka's Chief Minister's Office called inclusion in the Ninth Schedule **"the only permanent safeguard against future legal challenges"** to the 56% SC/ST quota law.

The Ninth Schedule does protect a law from most challenges. But in 2007 a nine-judge Bench of the Supreme Court held that **laws added to it after April 24, 1973, can still be struck down** if they violate fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure. So the protection is not permanent, and it is not automatic. Karnataka also has to get Parliament to pass a constitutional amendment first, and the Centre told Parliament on July 25, 2026, that it would defer the request while the matter was before the High Court.

## The evidence

### 1. What the state said

The release, as carried in reports on November 11, 2026, said Karnataka had filed a special leave petition against the High Court verdict of October 12, 2026, which cut SC reservation from 17% to 15% and ST reservation from 7% to 3%. It described the Ninth Schedule as the only permanent safeguard and asked why the 19 Lok Sabha MPs from the BJP and the JD(S) have not moved an amendment.

### 2. What the Supreme Court held in Coelho

In I R Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, decided on January 11, 2007, a Bench of nine judges looked at Article 31B, which gives laws in the Ninth Schedule protection from challenge. The Court held that for laws placed there after **April 24, 1973**, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, the protection is not complete. Such a law can be tested against the fundamental rights that make up the basic structure, among them equality and the freedoms in Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21. A law that violates them can be struck down even though it is in the Schedule.

### 3. How the Schedule is actually used

The best-known case is Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation law, which Parliament placed in the Ninth Schedule through the Constitution (Seventy-sixth Amendment) Act, 1994, after the Supreme Court's Indra Sawhney ruling of 1992 had set the 50% ceiling. It took a constitutional amendment to do so. That route does not bind the Court to uphold a law added later; Coelho keeps it open to review.

### 4. Where the Karnataka request stands

The Assembly passed a resolution on December 18, 2025, asking the Centre to include the 56% quota. According to the state's own release, a minister said in 2022 that the quota would be placed in the Schedule, but the then government issued an ordinance and a law instead. On July 25, 2026, the Centre told Parliament the request would be deferred because the matter was before the High Court. No amendment has been introduced.

## Why this claim spread

The release came the day the state announced its appeal, and it put the blame on the Centre and on the BJP and JD(S) MPs from Karnataka. The Ninth Schedule is widely understood as a safe harbour for quota laws, and the claim fits that belief. What the state's line leaves out is that the Court has kept the right to look inside the Schedule, so an amendment would improve the quota law's position without ending the legal fight.
