## What we found

The count is right and the comparison is fair as far as it goes, so the claim is mostly true. Both Houses were adjourned sine die on **August 13, 2026**. By Monday, November 9, neither had been prorogued, which is 88 days, one more than the 87 days of 2008. Jairam Ramesh said so on X that day. The part we cannot confirm is his reason: that the delay is meant to give Home Minister Amit Shah time to assemble a two-thirds majority for the delimitation Bills.

## The evidence

### 1. The days add up

Both Houses were adjourned on August 13. Counting forward, August has 18 more days, September 30, October 31, and November 9 makes **88**. By November 12, when our own report covered the plan for a special Session, the figure was 91. Reports on November 9 gave the same count and said the previous record of 87 days was set in 2008.

### 2. The Constitution sets no limit on this gap

Article 85(2) says the President "may from time to time prorogue the Houses or either House". Parliament's handbook explains the six-month rule of Article 85(1): **no more than six months** may pass between the last sitting of one Session and the first sitting of the next. It sets no date by which a House must be prorogued, notes that prorogation usually follows adjournment sine die by two to four days, and gives the Lok Sabha's 1987 session as an example of a House adjourned sine die on May 12, reconvened by the Speaker and prorogued only on September 3. The 76 days the minister cited for 1987 appear to count from that reconvened sitting, not from May 12; we could not find its date to check. Reports made the same point: staying unprorogued is not, by itself, a constitutional or procedural impropriety.

### 3. The 87 days of 2008 is the figure the government itself uses

Rijiju's letter of October 19 to Mallikarjun Kharge said the Houses had stayed unprorogued for long stretches before, naming 87 days in 2008, 76 days in 1987 and 54 days in 1989. Ramesh and the minister therefore agree on the old record. In 2008 the Lok Sabha sat on July 21 and 22 for the confidence vote, was kept unprorogued, sat again on October 17 and again later. The 87 days is the gap between two sittings. The 88 in 2026 is days since the last sitting, with no later sitting yet. So the two figures measure different things. Ramesh himself says the 2008 gap is not the same case, because all parties agreed to it.

### 4. What the delay allows

Reports say an extended sitting of an unprorogued Session needs at least two days' notice, and a fresh Session at least 14. That is the practical effect Ramesh points to: the government can recall the House quickly. It is also the effect Rijiju accepts when he defends the arrangement. Whether it is being used for a purpose is separate. Officials have told reporters they expect a special Session on November 24 or 25, and no notice has been issued.

## Why this claim spread

The April 17 defeat of the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill left the question of numbers open, and the government's talk of a special Session to bring it back made the unprorogued House look deliberate. Congress has used the record to argue that the delay is a tactic. The government has answered with precedent and the plain text. Both are partly right: the days are a record, the rule is silent, and the motive can only be tested when the Houses are called.
