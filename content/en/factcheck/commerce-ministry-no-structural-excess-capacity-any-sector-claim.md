## What we found

The Department of Commerce said on the night of November 25, 2026, that "India does not have structural excess capacity in any sector." The line came in a short government statement issued hours after the US Trade Representative named India among 14 economies whose practices it finds actionable under Section 301 of the US Trade Act of 1974. **The "any sector" part does not hold** on the government's own numbers. Steel supports the denial. Solar modules do not.

India's statement also says the notice is under examination and that trade concerns should be settled inside the India-US bilateral trade agreement talks, which it wants concluded early. Those are positions, not figures. Only the "no excess capacity" sentence can be tested.

## The evidence

### 1. What USTR found

On November 25 USTR said it had determined that the acts, policies and practices of 14 economies, India among them, are unreasonable, burden US commerce and are actionable under Section 301(b). The inquiry began on March 11, 2026, and drew nearly 140 witnesses and 1,070 comments. For India it lists five grounds, among them "industrial plans to expand manufacturing", inadequate labour protections, financial repression and market access barriers, and names **pharmaceuticals and medical devices, steel, solar and ICT** as sectors where the effects are especially true. It proposes tariffs "of up to 25 percent on all goods of India", falling to 17 percent if India signs a trade agreement. Comments and hearing requests are due on January 5, 2027, and hearings begin on January 25.

### 2. Steel: the numbers fit the denial

The Ministry of Steel puts India's crude steel capacity at **220.4 million tonnes in 2025-26** and output at 170.15 million tonnes. That is about 77%, a gap but not an idle industry. Finished steel consumption was 164.36 million tonnes. More telling, the same overview says India became a net importer again in 2026-27: from April to July 2026 it exported 2.29 million tonnes and imported 2.77 million, **net imports of 474,000 tonnes**. A country with a structural surplus would be shipping out more than it takes in. The ministry projects capacity of 300 million tonnes by 2030-31, which is a plan, not a present surplus.

### 3. Solar modules: the numbers do not

A PIB release of August 2, 2026 says India's solar installed capacity was 162.15 GW at the end of June and that **44.61 GW was added in 2025-26**. The same release puts module manufacturing capacity at about 172 GW on March 31, 2026, up from 2.3 GW in 2014. That is **nearly four times (3.9 times) a year's installations**. Some modules go to exports and some capacity is not fully built or used, so this is not proof of a glut, and the release is silent on utilisation. But a government that says no sector has excess capacity has to explain why one it promotes has capacity several times its home demand.

### 4. What "structural" means

USTR's page does not define structural excess capacity, and neither does India's statement. Its case is about conduct: industrial strategies, support and market barriers that it says create and sustain surplus production. A utilisation rate for one sector therefore cannot settle the matter either way. That is why the verdict is "misleading" and not "false": the denial is a legal and political position stated as a plain description of the economy, and the plain description fails in at least one named sector.

## What this does not settle

We did not check pharmaceuticals, medical devices or ICT, the other sectors USTR names, nor sectors it did not name. We have no all-manufacturing utilisation figure, because the Reserve Bank's latest survey could not be opened. Nor do we judge whether the proposed tariffs are justified, a question for the hearings in January. Whether USTR's list of India's policies amounts to "structural" excess capacity is for the proceeding, and the Government of India's formal reply will come through the comment process.
